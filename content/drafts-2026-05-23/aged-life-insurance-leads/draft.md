# Aged Life Insurance Leads: The Operator's Guide to Sub-Verticals, Pricing, and ROI in 2026

There isn't one aged life insurance lead market. There are three.

Term, final expense, and indexed universal life are different consumers, different sales cycles, different commission structures, and — the part most agents miss — different aged-lead economics. The 55-year-old who filled out a term life quote form and the 69-year-old who requested a final expense quote are not the same prospect. Treating them as interchangeable inventory is how agents underbuy one market and overpay for the other.

This guide covers all three sub-verticals: what aged life insurance leads actually are, what they cost in 2026, why life insurance leads hold conversion value longer than almost any other aged inventory, and how to work them in a way that's both operationally effective and compliant.

## What aged life insurance leads are (and what makes them different)

An aged life insurance lead is a consumer who filled out an internet form requesting a life insurance quote — term coverage, final expense, IUL, or some combination — and whose record was sold to real-time buyers when it came in, then released into the aged marketplace typically between 15 days and 12 months later.

The data is the same record that sold for $20-75 the day it was generated. What changed is the price and the competition. The original buyers — three to six of them typically — called aggressively in the first week, got stonewalled by a prospect who was overwhelmed and on the DNC radar of everyone who called, then moved on. By day 30, those buyers have given up. The lead is still sitting there, same contact information, same original intent.

The structural difference from other aged lead categories is time decay. In auto insurance, the prospect has a renewal date every six months — a 90-day-old auto lead may have already bought and isn't shopping anymore. In mortgage purchase, the home transaction either happened or didn't. Life insurance doesn't work that way.

The need that drove a 67-year-old to request a final expense quote doesn't expire in 90 days. Neither does the wealth-protection concern that drove a 48-year-old to request an IUL illustration. The aging demographic only increases urgency over time. This is why aged life insurance leads — correctly worked — hold conversion value at 6, 9, and 12 months in a way that aged auto or aged mortgage leads generally don't.

The spreads look enormous because they are. The 20x gap between a live-transfer final expense lead ($65) and an aged final expense internet-form lead ($1.25) doesn't mean the live transfer is 20x better — it means the live transfer is 20x more expensive and requires speed-to-lead infrastructure most independent agents don't have.

The direct mail response final expense tier at $8–25 is worth calling out separately. It's expensive for aged inventory, but it converts differently from internet-form aged leads because the consumer took an offline action to request the quote. That behavioral signal commands a premium, and the conversion rate justifies it for experienced FE agents.

For the purpose of this guide, the core analysis is internet-form aged leads across all three sub-verticals, because that's where the volume lives.

## Term life: the tightest economics of the three

Term is the hardest sub-vertical to run profitably on aged internet-form leads, and understanding why helps you allocate budget correctly.

Commission context: first-year commissions on term run $400–$2,000 depending on face amount, health class, and carrier. Monthly premiums are low — $20–$80 for a healthy 35-year-old on $500K of 20-year term. The model works on volume.

The aged math: at $10/lead (15–85 day shared) and a 2% effective conversion rate on contacts (which is achievable with a strong 10-touch cadence), cost per closing runs $500. On a $1,200 FYC policy, gross margin after lead spend is $700. That works, but the ceiling is low — one bad month of lead quality or contact rate wipes the margin.

The other issue is conversion decay. Term life buyers are rate-shoppers. A 60-day-old term lead has a higher probability of having already bought than a 60-day-old FE or IUL lead, because the term decision resolves faster (it's essentially a commodity product comparison). That doesn't make term aged leads a bad buy — it means you should expect lower contact-to-conversion rates than FE or IUL, and price your budget accordingly.

Where term aged leads work best: as a volume entry point for multi-product agencies. The agent who sells term first and converts to IUL or whole life later on a multi-year relationship does fine on aged term economics. The agent who runs term as a standalone high-volume telesales operation has tighter math.

## Final expense: where aged leads have the highest ROI

Final expense is the sweet spot of the three sub-verticals for aged internet-form leads, and it's not particularly close.

Commission context: FY commissions run 110–120% of annual premium at most carriers. Monthly premiums are $30–$80 for the typical FE buyer (ages 50–80, coverage amounts $5,000–$25,000). That's $500–$1,200 FYC per closed policy for a telesales agent. Door-knocking agents targeting higher face amounts can run $1,500–$2,500 FYC.

The aged math: at $1.25/lead (15–85 day shared internet-form) with a $1,500 budget, you buy 1,200 leads. A 15-touch cadence over 30 days produces roughly 25% contact rate = 300 conversations. At 3% conversion on contacts (conservative for an experienced FE telesales agent), you close 9 policies. At $700 average FYC, gross revenue is $6,300. Net of lead spend: $4,800. Cost per closing: $167.

Run the same $1,500 against real-time shared FE internet-form at $20/lead: 75 leads, 60% contact rate = 45 conversations, but now you're competing with 3–5 other agents who got the same lead at the same time. Effective conversion on real-time shared FE is often 4–6% of contacts because the prospect is overwhelmed and defensive. 45 conversations × 5% = 2.25 closings × $700 = $1,575. Net of lead spend: $75. Cost per closing: $667.

Aged wins by a factor of 4 on cost per closing in this example. Even if you stress-test it — assume aged converts at only 1.5% instead of 3% — the aged agent still closes 4.5 policies (vs. 2.25 real-time) at a cost per closing of $333.

The underlying reason: FE buyers are seniors who are often overwhelmed and under-served. The agent who calls them at day 45 with patience and a genuine conversation wins the sale that the agent who called at day 2 with a high-pressure opener lost. Aged leads in FE are self-qualifying — the prospects who didn't buy from the first wave of callers are the ones who were waiting for the right conversation.

## IUL: the highest upside per lead

Indexed universal life is a different calculation entirely because the commission ceiling is so much higher.

Commission context: IUL FYC runs $3,000–$10,000+ on a single policy. Monthly premiums start at $300 and can run to $1,500 for affluent buyers. A single IUL closing from aged leads can fund 6–12 months of lead spend.

The aged math: $5/lead (15–85 day shared IUL internet-form). $2,000 budget = 400 leads. 25% contact rate over a 14-touch cadence = 100 conversations. At 1% conversion on contacts (IUL is consultative, multi-touch, rarely closes on first call): 1 closing. At $5,000 FYC: gross revenue $5,000, net of lead spend $3,000.

That math looks thin until you account for two things. First, the multi-touch pipeline. IUL leads that don't close in month one don't disappear — they enter a nurture sequence. A well-managed aged IUL pipeline has closings coming from leads that were 3, 6, and 9 months in the queue. Second, the real-time comparison: $50/lead on real-time IUL. $2,000 = 40 leads. Higher contact rate, but these prospects are often shopping for illustrations from multiple advisors simultaneously. A 3% conversion on 40 leads × $5,000 FYC = $6,000. Net of lead spend: $4,000.

Real-time wins on a single-month snapshot if you have the speed-to-lead infrastructure and the sales process to close quickly. Most independent producers don't — and the IUL sales cycle doesn't support it anyway. Aged IUL at volume is the right model for the solo advisor or small team running a pipeline-based consultative process.

## How to work aged life insurance leads

### Volume and cadence come first

The ROI math for aged leads only works at scale. A 14-touch sequence (phone + email + voicemail) over 21 days on 200 leads produces a different result than the same sequence on 1,200 leads. The law of averages doesn't operate on small samples.

Minimum viable test for each sub-vertical:
- Term: 500 leads (15–85 day)
- Final expense: 1,000 leads (15–85 day internet-form, or 100 leads of direct mail response)
- IUL: 300 leads (15–85 day)

Run the test for a full calendar month with a minimum of 8 touches before declaring a lead dead. Life insurance aged leads in particular require patience — the senior FE prospect who doesn't answer the first six calls will often answer the seventh if you're not aggressive in the voicemails.

### Filtering by age bracket

Newer isn't always better in life insurance. For final expense, the 86–365 day aged bracket often outperforms the 15–85 day bracket on cost per closing because the lower price point lets you buy 3–4x as many leads for the same budget. The conversion rate drops, but not proportionally to the price.

For IUL, the 15–85 day bracket is worth the price premium — IUL buyers are higher-intent and have shorter decision windows (relative to FE), so recency matters more.

For term, stay in the 15–85 day bracket. Term buyer intent decays faster than FE or IUL.

## Compliance: Mode A, Mode B, and what that means for life insurance leads

Life insurance leads fall under the same TCPA framework as other aged internet-form leads. The original consent was captured on the lead form. As of May 2026:

- The FCC 1:1 consent rule was **vacated** in January 2025 and formally repealed in August 2025 — meaning prior consent from the original form-fill is generally sufficient for outreach.
- Revocation-of-consent-for-all was delayed to **January 2027**, with individual revocation still required immediately on request.
- State mini-TCPAs remain active and are the higher risk layer: **FL (FTSA)**, **OK (OTSA)**, **WA (HB 1497)**, **MD**, and **TX** all have tighter state-level restrictions that can overlay federal rules.

**Mode A (Conservative):** Use only leads where the original consent clearly names your company or vertical. Rely on email and direct mail for re-engagement, reserve phone calls for leads who respond first. Lower volume, lower compliance risk.

**Mode B (Operator Standard):** Use aged internet-form leads with original form-fill consent. Maintain full DNC scrub, honor opt-outs within 24 hours, document consent chain. This is the standard operating mode for most professional aged-lead operators and is what the Aged Lead Operator's System teaches in the insurance vertical.

**Mode C (Not Recommended):** Any approach that ignores state mini-TCPAs, skips DNC scrubbing, or relies on ambiguous consent chains. The savings aren't worth the exposure, particularly in FL and WA where private rights of action exist.

Final expense deserves special mention: the senior demographic (50–80) is a higher-risk population from a state-regulatory standpoint. Florida's FTSA is particularly aggressive about calls to consumers 65+. If you're running FE in Florida at any volume, get TCPA counsel before you dial. ## The bottom line

The aged life insurance lead market is undersold as an acquisition channel because most agents who try it treat it as a single product. It isn't. Term, final expense, and IUL have fundamentally different economics, and the operator who understands those differences can build a cost-per-closing number that makes real-time leads look expensive by comparison.

For most independent agents and small teams:
- **Final expense aged internet-form** is the highest-ROI starting point — low cost, scalable volume, a prospect who responds to patience and expertise rather than speed-to-lead.
- **IUL aged** is the highest revenue-per-closing opportunity — start smaller, nurture longer, and let the pipeline build.
- **Term aged** works in a multi-product strategy, not as a standalone high-volume model.

The compliance layer is real but manageable at Mode B with proper consent documentation, DNC scrubbing, and state-level awareness. The agents who build a compliant, systematic aged-life-insurance operation in 2026 are buying into a channel with very little competition from anyone who knows what they're doing — because most of the agents who "tried aged leads once" never got to the test scale that makes the math work.

That's the edge. Use it.
