Final expense agents split into two camps. One buys real-time leads at $25-65 apiece, works a dozen or so each week, and burns through a $500 monthly budget in about two days. The other loads 400 aged leads into their dialer for the same $500, runs a 21-day cadence, and closes three or four policies by month-end.

The math isn't subtle. Neither is the resistance to aged leads among newer agents who've been told fresh leads close better.

They do — per contact. That's not the same as per dollar spent, which is the only number that matters when you're running a production business.

## What aged final expense leads are — and the two types that matter

Aged final expense leads are internet inquiries or direct mail responses that weren't converted by the original buyer. They get resold, usually at a steep discount, to agents willing to work a longer cadence.

The two types are genuinely different products with different use cases.

**Internet form aged leads** originated on comparison or lead-gen sites where the prospect submitted a request for final expense coverage. Someone bought them at real-time price, made a few calls, didn't convert, and moved on. You buy the remainder — typically 31-180 days after the original inquiry — at $0.25-$2 per lead depending on age and exclusivity.

**Direct mail response aged leads** started as physical mail campaigns. The prospect received a mailer and returned a reply card requesting coverage information. These leads run $8-$25 in the secondary market because the barrier to response was higher — this person held a pen and mailed something back. When original buyers had excess inventory or couldn't convert within their working window, those reply cards entered the secondary market.

Neither type is inherently better. They require different working approaches and fit different agent profiles, which is why most experienced FE operators run both.

Data included in both types: name, primary phone (often mobile-appended), email, date of birth, and ZIP. Better vendors append NCOA (National Change of Address) verification and run phone validation before delivery. A 75% callability claim from a reputable vendor means disconnected numbers have been scrubbed — a real difference in effective dials per batch.

## The 2026 pricing picture

Real-time final expense leads have climbed. Live transfers now run $50-$80 per call. Shared internet form leads range $15-$65 depending on vendor and exclusivity window. Those prices have moved up as the market has tightened — more agents competing for the same real-time supply.

Aged leads have stayed cheap — that's structurally guaranteed, because their value derives from the original buyer's decision not to continue working them, not from current market demand.

| Lead Type | Age | Price Range |
|---|---|---|

| Internet form | Real-time, exclusive | $30-$100 |

The 31-85 day internet form tier is the highest-volume entry point for most FE operators. At $1.25 per lead — midpoint of that range — a $500 budget buys 400 leads. Direct mail response aged at $15 per lead gets you 33 leads with a substantially different working profile. That gap explains why the two types are not interchangeable budget items.

## The math — two agents, same $500 budget

Same vertical. Same product. Same week. Different lead source.

**Agent A — real-time shared internet form**

Budget: $500. Lead cost: $30 per lead. Leads purchased: 16. Contact rate on first attempt: 35% — about 6 contacts. Close rate: 15% of contacts (fresh leads, highest per-contact rate). Closings: 0.9 — call it 1 policy. Average FYC: $800. Revenue: $800. Net after lead spend: $300. Cost per closing: $500.

**Agent B — aged internet form, 31-85 day shared**

Budget: $500. Lead cost: $1.25 per lead. Leads purchased: 400. Contact rate over 21-day cadence: 30% — 120 contacts. Close rate: 2.5% of contacts (lower per-contact rate, but volume carries it). Closings: 3. Average FYC: $800. Revenue: $2,400. Net after lead spend: $1,900. Cost per closing: $167.

Read those numbers again. Agent A spent $500 to net $300. Agent B spent $500 to net $1,900. Agent B's cost per closing is one-third of Agent A's, and closes three times as many policies.

The objection you'll hear is that aged leads are terrible quality. On a per-contact basis, that critique isn't wrong — fresh leads do convert at a higher rate per dial. But Agent B doesn't need them to. At a 24x price premium, the math for real-time leads has to be nearly perfect for the economics to hold up. The math for aged leads has room to breathe.

## Where aged FE leads come from

The supply chain matters if you care about data quality — and you should.

Internet form aged leads originated on comparison sites, insurance quote pages, and affiliate lead-gen networks. The prospect submitted their information and the lead was sold into the real-time market. When the original buyer stopped working it, the lead went back to the vendor or into a secondary aggregator's inventory. The time between original inquiry and your purchase is the "lead age" — 31 days, 85 days, 120 days, and so on.

Direct mail response leads started with a physical mailer — typically targeted to homeowners aged 55-80 in specific ZIP codes — and the prospect mailed back a reply card. That demographic skew toward homeowners, rural markets, and fixed-income households is exactly the final expense buyer profile. Original buyers had surplus inventory, reached their calling capacity, or couldn't close within their working window. Those cards entered the secondary market.

The friction gap between the two types is real. Filling out a web form takes 90 seconds. Finding a stamp and mailing a reply card takes 10 minutes and a trip to the mailbox. That's why direct mail response aged leads cost $13 more per lead and close at a higher rate per contact. You're paying for demonstrated intent.

## Working aged FE leads — the 21-day cadence

Aged leads don't work on a speed-to-lead model. Real-time leads reward the fastest caller; aged leads reward the most persistent one. The goal isn't to beat a competitor to the first dial — it's to be the first agent to reach a prospect who's been called maybe twice and given up on.

An effective 21-day aged FE cadence covers three weeks and three channels.

**Week 1 (Days 1-7):** Three calls — early morning (7:30-9 AM), mid-morning (10-11:30 AM), and early evening (4:30-6 PM). One SMS on day 2. One email on day 4. The first call gets a voicemail message; subsequent calls vary the time of day. Don't leave voicemail on every attempt — two max in the first week.

**Week 2 (Days 8-14):** Two calls plus one SMS. If your CRM tracks email opens, prioritize calling leads who opened the day 4 email — they're still thinking about it. This week produces a disproportionate share of second-chance conversations.

**Week 3 (Days 15-21):** One final call and one "breakup" email. Frame the email as a last attempt: "I've tried a few times to reach you about the coverage information you requested. If you're still interested, I'm here. If not, no problem — I won't reach out again." That framing pulls callbacks from people who've been avoiding unknown numbers.

The opening script on first contact matters. Prospects who don't recognize your number aren't hostile — they're uncertain. Lead with their action, not yours: "I'm following up on your request for information about final expense coverage" outperforms "This is John from ABC Life." They anchor to something they did, not something you're selling.

How you document consent for the working cadence depends on your compliance posture. Mode A operators — conservative, using only refreshed consent — will verify prior express written consent before the first dial. Mode B operators rely on the original form consent, which is federally valid following the FCC 1:1 rule repeal in August 2025. The right mode for your production is a compliance question, not a lead-buying question — [Henson Legal](https://www.henson-legal.com/) is the TCPA counsel we recommend for operators working aged leads at scale.

## Compliance for senior-focused prospecting

Final expense targets a specific demographic — predominantly adults aged 55-80 on fixed incomes — which attracts regulatory attention that general life insurance prospecting doesn't face. The rules that matter in 2026:

**TCPA federal baseline:** Prior express written consent governs autodialed and prerecorded calls to wireless numbers. The FCC 1:1 consent rule that would have required per-seller consent was vacated by the 11th Circuit in January 2025 and formally repealed in August 2025. One-to-many disclosed consent on the original inquiry form is sufficient at the federal level.

**Consent revocation:** Consumers may revoke consent via any reasonable means — a spoken request on an informational call counts. Honor revocations within 10 business days. Do not re-add revoked contacts to an active cadence.

**State mini-TCPAs:** Florida's FTSA, Oklahoma's OTSA, and Washington's HB 1497 impose requirements beyond federal TCPA — higher per-violation penalties, expanded coverage to SMS, and stricter consent standards in some cases. FE agents dialing FL prospects specifically need a compliance review before scaling. The FTSA's per-violation exposure can exceed a month's lead budget on a single complaint.

**DNC scrubbing:** Aged leads should be scrubbed against the national registry before the first dial. Ask your vendor directly: do you scrub before delivery, or do you deliver raw and expect the buyer to scrub? The answer determines your liability if a complaint arises.

Compliance isn't separate from economics here — a clean, scrub-verified list is a more callable list. Removed disconnected numbers and DNC registrants mean more of your cadence time lands on actual prospects.

## Internet form aged vs. direct mail response aged — which is right for you

The simplest framework is budget and working style.

**Start with internet form aged if:**
- Monthly budget is under $500 (meaningful test batches start at $100-200)
- You're building or maintaining a high-volume phone cadence
- You want to test multiple geographies, age windows, and filters before committing
- You're newer to aged leads and want a forgiving volume-to-close ratio

**Add direct mail response aged when:**
- You've proven your internet form aged conversion rate and want a higher-quality complement
- Your agency does field sales alongside phone work — DM responders are substantially more receptive to in-person follow-up
- Average FYC is above $1,000 (the per-lead math supports the higher cost)
- You want to diversify your supply chain away from a single internet-form vendor

Most experienced FE operators run both simultaneously. The DM response leads close faster and at higher rates per contact, smoothing out the variable close weeks that come with pure internet form volume. The internet form aged leads provide the volume floor needed for consistent monthly production.

## What this means for you

If you're running real-time FE leads exclusively, the most direct test is straightforward: take 10-20% of your current monthly lead budget, buy 200-400 aged internet form leads, and run the 21-day cadence. Compare cost per closing after 30 days.

The cadence requires discipline. Aged leads do not self-select into your calendar — you work through the stack methodically over three weeks, not three days. That operational difference separates operators who see 3x ROI from agents who try aged leads once, call each lead twice in a week, and declare them worthless.

Action items for this week:

- Calculate your current cost per closing from real-time leads (total lead spend ÷ closings that month)

- Confirm your vendor scrubs against DNC before delivery, or run the scrub yourself
- Track contact rate, conversation rate, and close rate separately — not just total closings
- After 30 days, compare your cost per closing to your real-time baseline
