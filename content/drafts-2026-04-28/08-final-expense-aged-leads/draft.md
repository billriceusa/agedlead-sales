# Aged Final Expense Leads: The Operator's Guide to Pricing, Cadence, and ROI

Every final expense agent eventually runs into the same math problem. Real-time leads cost $15 to $65 per record. A realistic close rate on those leads — assuming decent follow-up — is 8 to 12 percent. That puts your cost per closing somewhere between $125 and $800, depending on source and how tight your operation is. For agents writing $500 to $1,500 in first-year commission per sale, the margins range from "fine" to "barely worth it" to "actively losing money on every close."

Aged final expense leads solve the cost side of that equation. They don't solve it with some trick or special technique — they solve it because the economics of aged leads are built around a single structural advantage: the original buyer already paid for acquisition, gave up, and left the prospect untouched. You're buying the upside at a fraction of the original cost.

The problem is that most agents who test aged FE leads apply the wrong framework. They treat them like a slower version of real-time leads, expect to work through a batch in two days, and declare the experiment a failure when results don't match their real-time benchmarks. That failure is a strategy problem, not a product problem.

## Why final expense aged leads are different from other aged leads

Final expense leads share one structural trait that makes them more forgiving than almost any other aged-lead category: the prospect's need doesn't go away.

Compare that to a mortgage lead. A refinance lead goes stale in a meaningful way — rates move, the borrower closes with someone else, or their equity situation changes. A prospect who was shopping a refi in January may be completely out of play by March. There's genuine expiration built into the product.

A final expense prospect who filled out a form in January and never got enrolled is still a 65-year-old who needs coverage in June. The coverage gap didn't close. The emotional driver — protecting the family from funeral and burial costs — is exactly where it was. If anything, it's grown more urgent. The need doesn't age the way interest rate intent ages.

That's the first thing that separates aged FE from aged mortgage leads. The lead ages. The buyer doesn't.

The second difference is volume and filter depth. Final expense is a high-activity vertical — direct mail campaigns, lead gen sites, Facebook forms, Medicare cross-sell inquiries — which means substantial supply cycles into the aged marketplace at low cost. Filters are broad: age range, geography, coverage amount, income tier, lead source type. Deep supply with wide filters keeps prices low and gives operators real selection control.

| Real-time direct mail response (exclusive) | 0–7 days | $8–$25 |
| Real-time live transfer | Immediate | $50–$80 |
| Aged internet form | 30–90 days | $0.62–$1.88 |
| Aged internet form | 90–180 days | $0.25–$0.75 |

That is not a typo. A 30-to-90-day aged FE internet lead runs under $2 per record at the median. A 90-to-180-day lead runs under $1.

The gap between those numbers and a real-time lead is a 10x to 40x price differential. For real-time leads to win on cost-per-closing, they would need to convert at 10 to 40 times the rate of aged leads. They don't. Not even close.

There's a common objection here: "Aged leads have terrible contact rates." That's true if you work them wrong. Properly run — with the right cadence, the right number of touches, and basic list hygiene — aged FE leads produce contact rates that narrow the effective conversion gap significantly. The math below shows what this looks like in practice.

## The A vs. B comparison

Same $500 monthly lead budget. Same skill level. Two different approaches.

**Agent A buys real-time shared internet leads at $30 each.**

$500 ÷ $30 = 16 leads per month.

Contact rate for shared internet leads: 30–40%. The lead was sold to 3 to 5 other agents the same minute it came in. Call it 35%.

Contacts made: 16 × 0.35 = 5.6 contacts.

Close rate on contacts for a trained FE agent: 20–30%. Call it 25%.

Closings: 5.6 × 0.25 = 1.4 closings per month.

First-year commission at $800 average: $1,120.

Cost per closing: $500 ÷ 1.4 = **$357**.

**Agent B buys 60-to-90-day aged internet leads at $1.25 each.**

$500 ÷ $1.25 = 400 leads per month.

Contact rate over a proper multi-touch cadence: 15–20%. Call it 17%.

Contacts made: 400 × 0.17 = 68 contacts.

Close rate on contacts — lower than real-time, since the prospect has already been called at least once — is 12–18% for a trained agent. Call it 15%.

Closings: 68 × 0.15 = 10.2 closings per month.

First-year commission at $800 average: $8,160.

Cost per closing: $500 ÷ 10.2 = **$49**.

Agent B spent the same $500. Agent B wrote roughly 7x more premium. The difference is entirely in the math.

A few honest caveats before you run this past your manager: Agent B's 17% contact rate assumes a structured cadence — not one call and a voicemail. Working 400 leads in a month requires a CRM and a power dialer; you can't do it manually and hit those numbers. And the first month usually underperforms while you're calibrating the list's quality and your dialer settings. But the directional advantage is real and consistent. Operators who run aged FE leads at scale — with systems in place — consistently report cost-per-closing in the $40–$80 range once their cadence is dialed in. ## Age brackets and what to expect

Not all aged leads perform identically. Here is how to think about the brackets specifically for final expense.

**30–60 days.** Closest to real-time in behavior — and priced accordingly, though still a fraction of fresh-lead cost. The prospect has probably spoken with at least one agent already and has formed an opinion. Contact rate is highest in this bracket. Your opening needs to establish a clear reason to re-engage: a coverage comparison, a rate that changed, or a gap the first agent missed. These leads move fast; work them within the first week of purchase.

**60–90 days.** The sweet spot for most operators. The original lead rush has died down. The prospect either didn't enroll or enrolled and has buyer's remorse — a statistically common outcome in FE given the number of doorknock and phone campaigns in this vertical. Contact rate is still solid, the prospect is more open to a real conversation, and the "I want to make sure you got the best option" positioning lands naturally. Price is meaningfully lower than the 30-day bracket.

**90–180 days.** Volume is high and price is lowest. Contact rates drop, but per-contact economics remain strong because the cost basis is so low. Some agents use 90-to-180-day records specifically for email warming before dialing — a short sequence of 2 to 3 educational emails before the first call increases answer rates and shortens the initial call conversation.

**Beyond 180 days.** Still viable for operators with automated cadences and strong list hygiene. Works best in rural or secondary markets where the initial lead rush was thinner and fewer agents competed for the original record. The expectation has to be calibrated accordingly — long cadence, low cost basis, high volume.

Practical implication: start with the 60-to-90-day bracket, establish your baseline contact and close rate, then decide whether the 30-day premium bracket or the 90-day value bracket makes more sense for your operation's capacity.

## The cadence that fits senior buyers

Final expense prospects are predominantly 55 to 80 years old, often on fixed income, and frequently skeptical of unsolicited contact. The cadence that works for them is different from what you'd run on a mortgage refi list.

**Call windows matter more than in other verticals.** The standard 9 AM to 8 PM window is technically compliant, but peak contact rates for seniors run between 10 AM and noon and again from 2 PM to 5 PM local time. Evening calls get answered less frequently. Program your dialer to concentrate dials in those windows.

**Persistence beats speed.** Unlike auto insurance or mortgage, where first-call advantage is significant, FE prospects often need 4 to 6 touches before they'll have a real conversation. They're used to being called. The agents who stand out are the ones who call day 1, follow up day 3, send a brief email day 5, call again day 7, and continue the sequence through day 21 — with a message that evolves at each step. The first call establishes presence. The fourth call is often where the conversation happens.

**Voicemails should be short and specific.** "This is [Name] — I'm calling because you requested information about final expense coverage. I have some updated options in your area and want to make sure you've seen them. I'll try you again Thursday at 10 AM." Give a specific callback time and then call at exactly that time. Seniors track follow-through. An agent who says 10 AM and calls at 10 AM already has a credibility advantage over everyone who called and disappeared.

**The opening line for aged FE needs to acknowledge the time gap.** "You requested information about final expense coverage a few months back — I want to make sure you had a chance to review all your options before making a decision." That opening is different from the generic re-intro most agents use. It names the original inquiry, frames your call as a service rather than a pitch, and invites a conversation. ## Compliance: what you need to know before dialing

Final expense marketing to seniors requires strict TCPA compliance. The FCC's 1:1 consent rule was vacated by the 11th Circuit in January 2025 and formally repealed in August 2025 — which simplified some of the consent landscape for shared leads at the federal level. But state-level laws in FL, OK, WA, MD, and TX impose additional requirements, and FCC rules effective 2024 mean that AI voice tools require prior express written consent regardless of federal TCPA status.

The cleanest path is simple: work only aged leads where the original form captured written consent for marketing follow-up. Reputable vendors document this. Ask before you buy. ## Three metrics to track from day one

**Contact rate.** Target 15–25% over a 90-day cadence. Below 15% suggests a list quality issue or a cadence problem. Above 25% on older records means you've got a tight filter and a well-configured dialer.

**Conversations-to-application rate.** For FE — simplified underwriting, single product, clear emotional need — this should be 20–30% of real conversations. Below 15% consistently means the scripting or qualification approach needs adjustment.

**Cost per closing.** Track this by lead age bracket and by vendor. The sustainable range for aged FE is $40–$80 per closing. Above $150 and you're giving back most of the economic advantage over real-time leads. The [ROI calculator](/calculators/roi) lets you plug in your actual numbers against any lead cost scenario.

## A practical starting point

If you're testing aged FE leads for the first time, here is a configuration that works:

- Budget: $500 for the first month.
- Lead age: 60–90 days, internet form.
- Volume: 300–400 records at $1.25–$1.50 per record.
- Filters: age 55–75. Start with broad geography — narrowing filters too early limits volume before you have baseline conversion data.
- Cadence: 6 touches over 21 days — calls on days 1, 3, 7, and 14; email on days 5 and 12. Text on day 2 only in states and contexts where your compliance review supports it.
- CRM: any lead management system that automates follow-up tasks. Without automation, the cadence falls apart at 300+ records per month.

After 30 days, review contact rate, conversation rate, and applications written. Adjust filters and cadence based on what the data shows — then scale what works.
