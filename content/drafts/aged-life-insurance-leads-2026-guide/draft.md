# Aged Life Insurance Leads: The 2026 Insurance Agent's Complete Buying and Working Guide

Aged life insurance leads are the most forgiving lead category in the industry. Auto leads go cold fast — six-month-old auto intent is often worthless because the person bought a policy. Mortgage leads expire with rate environments. But a final expense prospect who filled out a web form eight months ago? They're still 72 years old. Their need — to not leave their family with burial costs — hasn't resolved. The life event that drove the inquiry is still there.

That's the structural reason aged life insurance leads work when comparable aged leads in other verticals get marginal. It's not magic. It's life stage economics.

This guide covers the three sub-markets inside "life insurance leads," the pricing benchmarks for each, how to run the cost-per-policy math that actually decides whether aged or real-time wins for your operation, and how to work them in 2026 without running into TCPA compliance problems — especially in the senior demographic where the risk profile is highest.

## Three markets, not one

When agents ask "how much do aged life insurance leads cost?" they're asking the wrong question. The life insurance lead market breaks into three segments that have almost nothing in common: term life, final expense, and IUL. The pricing, the sales cycle, the contact dynamics, and the commission math are different in each. Treating them as one category is how agents end up buying the wrong leads at the wrong price.

**Term life** is a volume play. Mid-market consumers — working-age adults, often driven by a life event like a new mortgage or a second child — shop term on price. The premiums are low ($40-$120/month for a healthy 35-year-old with $500,000 coverage), which means the commission is also low. First-year commission (FYC) on a term case typically runs $500-$2,000, depending on face amount and carrier. That caps what you can rationally spend per close.

**Final expense** is its own category. Senior buyers, $30-$80/month premiums, simplified underwriting (no exam required), and first-year commissions running $500-$1,500 on a 110-120% target premium basis. The sales cycle is shorter than term — most final expense decisions close in two or three conversations — but the demographic (usually 50-85 years old) brings specific compliance considerations that don't apply to working-age term prospects.

**IUL (indexed universal life)** is a different business entirely. High-net-worth or near-HNW buyers, $300-$1,000+/month premiums, and first-year commissions that run $3,000-$10,000 or more per placed case. IUL is a consultative sale — multiple meetings, often involving a spouse, often involving an existing financial advisor relationship. The lead economics are inverted: you can spend dramatically more per lead and still clear a healthy margin per close.

The strategic implication: if you sell all three and you're buying one lead type, you're leaving money on the table in two sub-markets.

A few things stand out in that table. The aged final expense internet form is the most aggressively priced lead in the life market — sub-$2 per lead on the typical shared batch. The direct mail response aged exclusive is expensive relative to internet form but is a completely different animal: that prospect took an offline action, mailed back a card, and explicitly requested contact. The contact rate and conversion rate on direct mail response aged leads is 3-5x what you get on internet-form aged leads of the same age.

The IUL spread is the one most agents sleep on. A $2–$6 aged IUL lead, sourced from the same form-fill population that sold real-time at $40–$75, is competing against almost no one. IUL agents tend to be advisor-channel, referral-dependent, and slow to adopt lead buying. The aged IUL market is not crowded.

## The cost-per-policy math by sub-vertical

Sticker price isn't the metric that matters. Cost per placed policy is. Here's how that runs in each sub-market with realistic assumptions.

### Term life — the tight-margin sub-market

Assume a $5,000 monthly lead budget allocated entirely to aged term internet-form leads at $8 average. That's 625 leads.

At a realistic 25% contact rate and 3% contact-to-close rate on aged term, you place approximately 4.7 policies. Average FYC per term policy: $1,200. Gross commission: $5,640. Net after lead cost: $640.

Those numbers are not the reason to buy aged term. The reason is that term converts to IUL or whole life renewal. If 10% of your term clients upgrade or cross-purchase a whole life or IUL product in year 2, the lifetime economics improve substantially. Aged term works as acquisition — specifically acquisition at a cost-per-relationship that you can justify when you factor in the full policy lifecycle.

If you're selling term only, with no cross-sell plan, aged term at real-time prices has a thin margin. Aged term at sub-$10 pricing changes the math.

### Final expense — where aged leads shine

Final expense is where the case for aged internet-form leads is cleanest.

Two agents, each with $1,000 to spend on final expense leads:

**Agent A buys real-time internet-form final expense leads.** At $25 per lead, that's 40 leads. Contact rate on real-time FE: 40-50% (good, but you're competing with 3-5 other agents who got the same lead in the same 24 hours). Let's say 40% contact, 8% contact-to-close. Placed policies: 1.3. Average FYC: $900. Gross: $1,170. Net after lead cost: $170.

**Agent B buys aged internet-form final expense leads.** At $1.25 per lead, that's 800 leads. Contact rate on aged FE: 12-18% (lower than real-time, but you're the only one calling). Let's use 15%, 6% contact-to-close. Placed policies: 7.2. Average FYC: $900. Gross: $6,480. Net after lead cost: $5,480.

The assumptions on Agent B's side are conservative. Final expense agents who work aged leads with a disciplined multi-touch cadence — 8-10 contacts across phone, text, and mail over 21 days — routinely see contact rates in the 20-25% range on batches under 180 days old.

Read those numbers again. Same $1,000 budget. The math isn't close.

The reason final expense works so well with aged leads comes back to the life-stage economics mentioned at the top: a 68-year-old who requested burial insurance information four months ago hasn't changed their age, their health situation hasn't improved, and their family hasn't become less worried about covering final costs. The need is persistent.

### IUL — the sleeper category

IUL aged leads are the most underused segment in the market. At $2–$6 per lead, you can build a list of 500 pre-qualified, form-fill-originated IUL prospects for $1,000–$3,000.

The math: 1,000 aged IUL leads at $4 each = $4,000 in lead cost. At 20% contact rate, 250 conversations. At 2% conversion to placed IUL case, 5 sales. Average IUL FYC: $5,000. Gross: $25,000. Net: $21,000.

That's a 525% ROI on lead spend. IUL agents who work the referral-only model — and there are a lot of them — often don't believe these numbers until they run a test batch themselves.

The catch: IUL is a consultative product. You need to be comfortable having an education-first conversation that doesn't close in call one. If you're a final expense agent who power-dials 200 calls a day, IUL aged leads require a different gear — slower, more relationship-based, longer cadence. But the economics justify the investment in adapting that process.

## Why aged leads don't expire the way agents think

There's a common objection to aged life insurance leads: "They didn't convert the first time — why would they convert now?"

The objection gets the sequence wrong. The reason most life insurance aged leads didn't convert the first time is not that the prospect decided against buying. It's that the original real-time buyer — usually one of three to five agents — didn't reach them before they stopped answering calls from unknown numbers. Or they got overwhelmed with contacts and shut down. Or they were price-shopping, got quotes they needed to think about, and then forgot to follow up.

The need is still there. The budget is still probably there. What's missing is a second-chance contact from an agent who doesn't come across as the fourth person calling from the same lead source.

That's the second structural reason aged life leads work: by the time you're calling, the herd of real-time buyers has moved on. You're not the fourth voice. You're often the first voice they've heard in months — which means you get a fundamentally different reception.

The script for aged life leads has to acknowledge the gap without apologizing for it. Something in the vein of: "You filled out a request a few months back about [term/final expense/life coverage] — I know that was a while ago, and the timing may not have been right. I wanted to reach out now rather than never." That's a different conversation opener than what they heard the week after they submitted.

## 2026 compliance: what's changed for life insurance leads

The FCC 1:1 consent rule that would have required per-seller written consent on every lead form was vacated by the 11th Circuit in January 2025 and formally repealed by the FCC in August 2025. For most aged life insurance lead buyers, this means the one-to-many disclosed consent that was standard on most aggregator forms from 2023 forward is still a legally defensible basis for contact.

That's the federal picture. The state picture is more complicated.

**Florida FTSA (Florida Telephone Solicitation Act)** imposes a $500 per-call penalty — no cap — for telemarketing calls to Floridians made without prior express written consent that explicitly names the caller or authorizes calls from a specific category of callers. Final expense agents working Florida aged leads need to verify that each lead's consent language covers them specifically. The senior demographic in Florida is large, and the plaintiff's bar is active. Run Florida leads through your compliance review before you dial.

**Oklahoma OTSA, Washington HB 1497, Maryland, and Texas** all have state-level TCPA equivalents with varying consent requirements and penalty structures. If your lead batches span multiple states, the compliance analysis isn't uniform.

The cleanest path in 2026: treat final expense aged leads aimed at the 55+ demographic as requiring TCPA Mode A compliance — meaning written consent that names the calling party or category, verified before the first dial. This is the framework laid out in the Aged Lead Operator's System, and it's the position that [Henson Legal](https://www.henson-legal.com/) — the TCPA counsel referenced across that system — supports for high-volume senior outreach.

Mode A isn't the most aggressive approach, but it's the one that lets you scale without building legal exposure on every dial. When the state mini-TCPAs are this active, the cost of a compliant consent audit is trivially small relative to the per-call penalty in a bad state.

For consent revocation: the FCC April 2025 rule requires honoring any revocation request made by "any reasonable means" within 10 business days. Keep a running DNC list updated in your CRM and scrub against it before any batch dial.

## What to look for in an aged life insurance lead vendor

Not all aged leads are the same, and the differences matter more in life insurance than in some other verticals because the senior demographic drives the compliance risk.

**Data age transparency.** A reputable vendor tells you the exact age range of leads in a batch — not just "30-90 day aged" but the actual distribution. Final expense leads at day 31 work differently than final expense leads at day 89. You need to know what you're buying.

**Consent documentation.** Before placing a large order of senior-demographic leads, ask the vendor whether consent language is available for a sample of records. A vendor who can produce the original form-fill consent document is operating at a different standard than one who can't.

**Phone validation.** The best vendors run independent number verification on their inventory — checking for disconnected, ported, or reassigned lines — before you pay for them. A 20% bad-number rate on a batch isn't a feature, it's a data hygiene failure, and it's avoidable.

**Geographic filters.** If you're licensed in specific states and need to exclude Florida FTSA exposure, or you're only licensed in your home state, you need granular state filtering, not just region-level selection.

**No minimums and no contracts.** For testing a new aged lead vendor, buy a small batch — 100-200 records — before committing to volume. Any vendor that insists on a minimum purchase for your first order is asking you to absorb the due diligence risk they haven't earned the right to ask for yet.

The ROI on testing the right vendor before scaling is asymmetric: the downside of a test is $150-$250 in lead cost and a day of calls. The downside of scaling with the wrong vendor is $5,000 in bad data and four months of wasted time.

## The working cadence for aged life insurance leads

Aged life leads require more touches than real-time leads, but fewer touches than most agents think they need. Here's what the data shows for final expense and term aged internet-form:

- **Days 1-3:** Phone-heavy. 3 call attempts on day 1 (morning, midday, early evening). 2 on day 2. 1 on day 3 with a voicemail left if you haven't made contact.
- **Day 4:** Text message, short. Something like: "Hi [First Name], this is [Agent] — I tried calling a few times about your life insurance inquiry. Is this still something on your radar? Happy to talk at your convenience." No links, no lengthy pitch.
- **Days 7-10:** One more call attempt. If no contact, one email to any address in the record.
- **Day 14-21:** Final outreach cycle — one call, one voicemail. Voicemail at day 21 explicitly offers to close the loop: "If the timing isn't right, no problem at all — just let me know and I'll take you off my list."

This is 8-10 total touches over 21 days. It sounds like a lot, but the math shows contact rates plateau sharply after touch 8 on aged life leads. Extending the cadence to 60 days of weekly calls doesn't return proportional results — it produces diminishing returns and unnecessary compliance exposure.

For final expense agents working the senior demographic: earlier in the day is better. Retired prospects are most reachable 9-11 AM local time. The late-afternoon call patterns that work for working-age mortgage prospects don't transfer.

## Practical starting point

If you've been sitting on the fence about aged life insurance leads, here's the minimum viable test you can run in two weeks:

1. Pull 200 aged final expense leads (30-90 days, internet form, your licensed states) from a reputable vendor. Budget: $250 at $1.25/lead.
2. Run the 8-touch cadence described above.
3. Track: contact rate, conversations held, appointments set, applications taken, placed policies.
4. Calculate your actual cost per placed policy.

If the math works — and for most FE agents working a disciplined cadence, it does — scale up. If it doesn't, you've spent $250 to learn that rather than $5,000.

Final expense aged leads at sub-$2 per record are the closest thing to an asymmetric bet in the insurance acquisition market right now. The floor on your downside is a modest test budget. The ceiling, for an agent who works the cadence, is a cost-per-policy that most real-time lead buyers would be skeptical of if you told them.

The math doesn't ask for your opinion. It just asks you to run it.
