# Aged Life Insurance Leads in 2026: Term, Final Expense, and IUL Economics

Every life insurance agent eventually asks the same question: are aged leads worth it? The honest answer is: it depends entirely on which life insurance product you sell.

That's the thing most vendors won't tell you. "Aged life insurance leads" isn't one category — it's three, with three different price ranges, three different aging curves, and three different sets of CPA math. What works for a final expense telesales operation is structurally different from what works for an IUL producer. And term — the most widely sold life product — has its own economics that many agents get wrong.

This breaks down all three.

## The Three Sub-Markets You're Actually Choosing Between

Life insurance leads get lumped into one bucket, but the buyer profile, sales cycle, and economics are distinct enough that they need to be treated separately.

**Term life.** Consumer submits an internet form requesting a quote — typically $250K–$1M in face value, 20- or 30-year term. Premiums run $40–$120/month. First-year commission (FYC) ranges $500–$2,500 depending on face amount and carrier. High volume, relatively low commission ceiling per sale. The term market rewards speed-to-lead and dialing efficiency above almost everything else.

**Final expense (FE).** Consumer — typically 50–80 years old — requests a quote for burial or final expense coverage, usually $5K–$25K in face value. Monthly premiums run $30–$100. FYC is $500–$1,500 on a typical policy, often paid at 110–120% of target premium in the first year. Telesales-heavy and door-knocking-friendly. The senior demographic changes the compliance picture — more on that below.

**IUL (indexed universal life).** Consumer is researching permanent life insurance with cash value growth tied to a market index. Monthly premiums run $300–$1,000+. FYC can reach $3,000–$10,000+ per closed policy. Consultative sales cycle, often multiple conversations. The commission ceiling is high enough to absorb significant lead spend.

The reason the breakdown matters: the CPA each product can support determines which tier of aged leads makes sense to buy.

## Why Life Insurance Leads Hold Value at Older Ages

Here's the structural difference most agents don't think through: in most lead categories, the buying decision has an expiration date. A real-time mortgage lead resolves in 60–90 days — the person either locked a rate or stopped looking. An auto insurance lead resolves in days, because auto renews on a fixed schedule and the purchase window is tight.

Life insurance is different. The underlying need — protect the family, insure the mortgage, fund retirement, transfer wealth — doesn't expire on a 90-day timer. A 45-year-old who submitted a term life inquiry 180 days ago still has a spouse. Still has a mortgage. Still has kids in school. The need hasn't gone away; the original agents just gave up after two calls.

That's exactly the environment aged life leads exist in. The original buyer pool was typically sold to 3–5 agents in the first week, all of whom called aggressively and then abandoned the lead after the initial flurry. The consumer wasn't always ready to buy on that timeline. Six months later, they're often more ready — and nobody is calling them.

Final expense benefits from this dynamic most of all. FE prospects are seniors for whom the need is constant — at 70 years old, the question of funeral cost coverage isn't seasonal. IUL prospects are in a research phase that can stretch 6–18 months. Both categories age better than term, but term still works if the math is right.

## The Math — Three Agents, Same $1,000 Budget

Let's make this concrete.

**Agent A buys real-time term leads at $40/lead.** $1,000 = 25 leads. These were sold to 4 other agents on day one. Effective contact rate after speed-to-lead competition: 20%. That's 5 conversations. At 5% close rate on conversations: 0.25 closings. Most months: zero. Occasional month: one. FYC on a $500K term policy: $1,200. Cost per closing: $4,000 in a good month, infinite in a bad one.

**Agent B buys aged 31–85 day term leads at $8/lead.** Same $1,000 = 125 leads. No real-time competition — other agents gave up weeks ago. Cumulative contact rate across a 14-day systematic cadence: 25–30%. That's 31–37 conversations. At 2% close rate: 0.6–0.75 closings per batch. Not dramatic — but at $1,333–$1,667 cost per closing on a $1,200 FYC, Agent B is near breakeven. At 500 leads/month, the same math produces 2–3 closings. Volume changes everything.

**Agent C buys aged 31–85 day FE leads at $1.25/lead.** $1,000 = 800 leads. Cumulative contact rate across a systematic 10-day cadence: 25%. That's 200 conversations. At 2% close rate: 4 closings. FYC at $800 each: $3,200 gross against $1,000 spend. Cost per closing: $250.

Read those numbers again. Same budget. Three different structural outcomes.

Agent C isn't just doing better — the economics are in a different category. The FE aged lead model works because the per-lead cost is so low that even a modest contact-and-conversion rate produces profitable outcomes. The volume changes what the math can absorb.

## Working Aged Life Insurance Leads — What Actually Moves the Needle

The biggest mistake operators make with aged life leads is treating them like real-time leads — calling once, getting no answer, marking them dead.

Aged life leads require a cadence. The standard operating model: 8–10 dial attempts across 10–14 calendar days, supplemented with email and SMS where the lead opted into text contact. The contact window that converts: calls 3–6, not call 1. Most of the conversations that turn into closed business happen after the prospect has seen your number 2–3 times and decided you're not going away.

The opening script for an aged lead is different from a real-time script. You're not racing other agents. You're picking up a conversation the prospect had months ago. The frame that works: "You had looked into [term/final expense/IUL] a few months back — I wanted to follow up and see if that's still something you're thinking about." No apology for calling on old information. Just re-engaging a prior inquiry.

For final expense specifically: door-knocking the same aged leads you're calling dramatically improves conversion rates on the segment that doesn't answer the phone. The senior FE demographic responds to in-person conversations in a way that younger term leads don't. If you're running FE aged leads and not door-knocking your local ZIP codes, you're leaving the highest-conversion tier of your pipeline untouched.

For IUL: the cadence matters less than the qualification call. IUL leads need an early discovery conversation to understand whether the prospect's goal is tax-deferred retirement income, wealth transfer, or something else. Leads that convert to closed IUL policies typically go through 3–5 conversations. The aged lead advantage in IUL is competition — essentially zero. Nobody else is calling that 8-month-old IUL lead. You have the conversation entirely to yourself.

## The Compliance Frame

Aged life insurance leads carry TCPA consent from the original internet form fill. The consent travels with the record. Verify that your provider includes the original consent language and form-fill timestamp — any reputable aged lead source should provide this on request.

Two additional compliance notes specific to life insurance in 2026:

**Final expense and the senior demographic.** Florida's FTSA (Florida Telephone Solicitation Act) and Oklahoma's OTSA (Oklahoma Telephone Solicitation Act) impose tighter restrictions than federal TCPA in certain contexts. If your FE territory includes Florida — and for most telesales FE operations, it does — your cadence and consent documentation needs to account for state law, not just federal TCPA. [Henson Legal](https://www.henson-legal.com/) handles TCPA and state mini-TCPA compliance for insurance agents and is the firm I recommend for operators working the senior FE market.

**Revocation.** Current rules require honoring opt-out requests within 10 business days via any reasonable means. Your CRM needs to process STOPs and DNC additions in real time and suppress those records across your active lead batch. Don't skip this step — revocation tracking is the compliance piece operators most often let slide.

## Practical Playbook

The right entry point for each profile:

- **Start with FE aged leads if you want the best odds on a first batch.** Buy 500 aged FE leads (31–85 days) in your target state, run a 10-day 8-touch cadence, measure cost per closing over 60 days. Benchmark target: cost per closing under $500 on FYC of $700–$1,200.

- **If you sell term primarily**, buy in batches of 200–300 (31–85 days) and run a systematic cadence rather than one-time calling. Term aged leads require more dials per contact than FE. The economics tighten but remain positive with discipline.

- **IUL aged leads are the highest-ceiling play for qualified producers.** If you close 1 IUL policy per 1,000 dials, the math at $3–$5/lead is substantially positive. Most producers find contact rates of 25–30% on systematic cadences and close rates of 1–2% on contacts.

- **Track cost per closing, not cost per lead.** The per-lead number is irrelevant. What matters is what you actually spent to close a policy. Calculate it after every 30-day batch. Model your own numbers first with the [ROI calculator](/calculators/roi-calculator).

## What This Means for You

Aged life insurance leads work differently in each sub-vertical. That's both the challenge and the opportunity. Most agents lump them together, run one undifferentiated strategy, see mediocre results, and conclude aged leads don't work. The operators who break it down by sub-market — separate economics, separate cadences, separate scripts for term vs FE vs IUL — find that at least one of those sub-markets works well for their operation.

A few takeaways before you buy:

- Term, final expense, and IUL have different price ranges, different CPA math, and different optimal cadences. Treat them separately.
- The aging curve advantage in life insurance is structural — the underlying need doesn't expire on a 90-day timer the way it does in auto or mortgage purchase.
- Final expense aged leads at $0.62–$1.88/lead produce the most favorable economics for solo operators. Volume-to-conversion math is the best in the category.
- IUL aged leads have essentially zero competition. A systematic cadence on a batch of $4 IUL leads is one of the most underexploited channels in the insurance market.
- Compliance is not optional — especially in Florida and Oklahoma for the senior FE demographic.
