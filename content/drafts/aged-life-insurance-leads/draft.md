# Aged Life Insurance Leads: The Operator's Working System

Every life insurance agent eventually runs the math on lead costs. Real-time exclusive leads: $30–50 for term life, $15–65 for final expense. If you're closing 3% of what you buy, you need 34 contacts to write one application. At $40 per lead, that's $1,360 in lead cost per closed deal — before time, before a dialer, before overhead.

Aged life insurance leads solve the cost side of that equation. At $0.50–$2 each, the same closed deal costs $17–68 in lead acquisition. The math is overwhelmingly in favor of the aged approach. The catch — and it is a real one — is execution. Most agents buy aged leads, make two calls, get a lower contact rate than they expected, and conclude the channel doesn't work.

It works. The problem is the two calls.

## The Math — Agent A vs. Agent B

Same budget. Different approach. Here's what the numbers look like at scale.

**Agent A** puts $1,000 into 20 real-time exclusive term life leads at $50 each. He works them aggressively — calls within minutes, three attempts per lead. He sets 3 appointments, writes 1 policy. FYC on a healthy 42-year-old, $500K 20-year term: roughly $700. Lead cost per closed deal: $1,000.

**Agent B** takes the same $1,000 and buys 600 aged life insurance leads at $1.60 average (30–60-day mix, term and final expense, filtered by her active states). She runs a 7-day multi-channel cadence on every lead. Contact rate: 13%. That's 78 conversations. At a 6% close rate on contacted leads, she writes 4–5 policies — two final expense at ~$1,100 FYC, two term at ~$700 FYC. Rough production from the same $1,000: $3,700–$4,500.

Volume changes everything when per-unit cost drops by 95%. This is not a theoretical argument — it's the actual math that agents running aged-lead operations have tracked across thousands of campaigns.

The objection: "But aged leads have lower contact rates." True. Agent B's 13% still outperforms Agent A's implied 15% on contacts that matter, because she's working 30 times the volume. Effective conversion rate — policies per dollar spent — is the number that determines whether this business works. Contact rate is just one input.

## What "Quality" Actually Means for Aged Life Leads

Aged leads are not interchangeable. The filters that move close rates in the life vertical:

**Lead age range.** The 15–30-day window still carries intent close to real-time strength. The 30–60-day range is the volume sweet spot — price drops sharply, contact rates hold at 10–15%. The 60–90-day tier is the bulk-buy range: contact rates fall to 8–10%, but at $0.40–0.80 per lead, the economics still pencil with a disciplined cadence.

**Sub-vertical intent.** Final expense, term life, IUL, mortgage protection, and whole life each require a different conversation. A prospect who clicked a final expense ad at 67 years old is not having the same purchase consideration as a 38-year-old who Googled "how much life insurance do I need." Filter by product intent before you dial — or you're wasting your best opener on the wrong product.

**Consumer age.** Final expense conversations work best in the 60–75 band. Term life runs 30–55. IUL requires wealth signal — household income filter matters more here than in any other life sub-vertical. A $40,000-household-income prospect is not going to sustain a $400/month IUL premium regardless of how good the conversation goes.

**Data depth.** Quality aged life insurance lists carry 3–4 phone numbers per contact. Single-number lists wash out too fast to sustain the cadence below. Verify that email addresses are included — they're the second channel in your outreach sequence, not the primary, but the agents who skip email are leaving 15–20% of their contacts on the table.

Layering three filters — product intent, consumer age, geography — typically adds 20–30% to cost per lead. It can double effective close rates. Run your own numbers in the [ROI calculator](/calculators/roi) before deciding which filters to skip.

## The 7-Day Cadence That Separates Operators from Amateurs

The single biggest execution failure with aged leads: treating them like fresh leads in terms of contact volume. One call. Voicemail. Write it off.

Producers who build real production from aged life insurance leads run a structured 7-day multi-channel sequence:

**Day 1, morning:** First call attempt. If voicemail, leave 15 seconds: your name, that they requested life insurance information, you're following up to help. No pitch. No urgency theater.

**Day 1, afternoon:** Text message within 2 hours of the voicemail. One line: "Hi [Name], this is Bill Rice — left you a voicemail about your life insurance request. Text me back if you'd like to connect, no pressure." That's the whole message.

**Day 2:** Second call, different time of day than Day 1. If they work mornings, call at noon. If you called at 10 AM yesterday, try 4:30 today.

**Day 3:** Email. Reference the specific inquiry — "You requested information on life insurance coverage" — keep it two paragraphs, one CTA. No PDFs, no attachments. Subject line: "Still looking for coverage, [First Name]?"

**Day 5:** Third call. If no contact, try a different phone number from the lead record. Quality lists give you 3–4 numbers. Rotate.

**Day 7:** Final voicemail in the active sequence: "I don't want to keep bothering you — if you're still thinking about life insurance, I'm here. If not, I'll stop reaching out." This frame converts a meaningful percentage of non-responders because it removes the pressure and invites a reply.

Five to seven touches across three channels in seven days. Agents running this sequence consistently hit 12–18% contact rates on quality aged lists. Agents running two calls see 3–5%. The delta is entirely execution — not lead source.

After the 7-day active cadence, the lead moves to a 30-day slow drip: one call per month for three additional months, one quarterly email. The 90-day extended pipeline from a well-worked aged list typically yields another 30–40% of total production beyond the initial push.

## Sub-Vertical Economics: What You're Actually Working With

The ROI math runs differently depending on the product you're placing.

**Final expense** is the highest-volume play in aged life leads. Internet-form aged leads run $0.62–1.88 per lead; exclusive aged direct-mail responses (which carry stronger intent) run $8–25. FYC per closed policy: $500–1,500, depending on carrier and benefit amount. At a 6% close rate and $1.25 average lead cost, a 500-lead batch costs $625 and — worked properly — yields 7–8 contacted prospects who close, generating $3,500–12,000 in FYC. The per-lead cost is low enough that the economics hold even at modest close rates.

**Term life** runs a higher per-lead price at aged ($5–15, freshness-dependent) and lower FYC per policy if you're placing $500K term on a 40-year-old at $40/month ($400–700 FYC). This is a volume model — it requires high contact rates and a tight process. Agents winning with aged term leads use a power dialer and a strict maximum call threshold before rotating leads to a slower drip. For call scripts built around the term life conversation, see [aged lead scripts that actually work](/blog/aged-lead-scripts-actually-work-examples).

**IUL/universal life** is the smallest sub-market but the highest upside per deal. Aged IUL leads run $2–6 at typical quality — the CPS (cost per sale) on a commissioned basis can run $40–200, meaning you're paying $40–200 in lead cost to close one IUL case that generates $3,000–10,000+ in FYC. At 300 leads and $4/lead average, that's $1,200 in acquisition cost against potentially $15,000+ in production if you close two or three cases from 50 genuine conversations. The math works — if you can qualify on the call and have the product knowledge to run a consultative sale. ## Compliance: Which Mode You're Operating In

Working aged leads means working inside a regulatory environment that changed materially in 2025. You need to know your operating mode before you dial.

**Mode A (Conservative):** Documented consent records from the original opt-in, verified within 30 days of purchase. DNC scrubbed against the National Registry every 31 days. No AI-generated voice on any call. Fully compliant in all federal and state environments — including FL (FTSA), OK (OTSA), WA (HB 1497), MD, and TX, where state mini-TCPAs create independent private causes of action.

**Mode B (Operator Standard):** Documented consent available on request from your vendor — confirm before purchasing. Human or manual dialing. Monthly DNC scrub. Handles the 2026 federal environment cleanly, assuming your vendor's consent documentation is solid.

**Mode C:** Buying lists with no consent documentation, dialing with AI voice. This is not recommended. The FCC's February 2024 ruling classifies AI-generated voice as "artificial voice" under TCPA § 227(b)(1), requiring prior express written consent for marketing calls. The 11th Circuit's January 2025 vacation of the 1:1 consent rule helps at the federal level, but state laws are untouched. FL FTSA and OK OTSA create actionable claims independently. The exposure on Mode C is real.

One date worth marking: January 2027. The FCC's revocation-all rule — where revoking consent on any communication revokes all marketing permissions — was delayed to a January 2027 effective date. If you're building a large aged-lead operation, the compliance infrastructure to honor that rule needs to be in place before it hits.

## Before You Place an Order: The Vendor Checklist

Most bad aged-lead experiences trace back to one of five things. Ask any new vendor these before scaling:

1. **Where did these leads originate?** Specific sites and form types, not "online forms." A vendor who can't answer clearly has a sourcing problem.
2. **Can you produce a consent record for any individual lead on request?** If they can't, assume the consent documentation doesn't exist.
3. **What is your DNC scrub frequency?** The legal minimum is every 31 days. Quarterly scrubbing is not acceptable.
4. **What is your replacement policy?** Disconnected numbers and wrong contacts should be replaced. A vendor without a clear, written policy is cutting corners.
5. **What are your volume price breaks?** Meaningful price drops at 500, 1,000, and 5,000 leads are the mark of a vendor optimized for real buyers. Flat pricing at any volume is a warning sign.

Order 50–100 leads as a test before committing to a full budget. That's enough volume to evaluate contact rate, data accuracy, and how the vendor responds to a replacement request. It costs less than one wasted month on a bad source.

## Starting This Week

If you're building an aged life insurance lead operation from scratch, the sequence is:

- Set a $500 test budget. Buy 200–400 aged leads — 30–60-day range, life intent filtered to your active states. Mix term and final expense to see where your close rate is stronger.
- Load them into your CRM with clean status tracking: uncontacted / attempted / contacted / working / closed / not interested.
- Configure the 7-day cadence in your dialer or automation. Don't run this from memory — the cadence only works if it's automatic.
- Log every call outcome for the first 60 days. Contact rate and cost per closed deal are the two numbers that tell you whether to scale.
- At 60 days, run the math. If your cost per issued policy is below what real-time leads would cost, scale the budget. If not, diagnose the breakdown: was it lead quality, contact rate, or close rate? Each has a different fix.

The operators who build consistent production from aged life insurance leads aren't doing anything exotic. They run a repeatable system, track the numbers, and scale what works. The system is learnable. The numbers are achievable.
