# Aged Insurance Leads: Contact Rates, Cadence, and the 2026 Outreach System

Every insurance agent who's bought aged leads has had the same 72-hour experience. The records arrive. They look right — name, phone, email, the product they expressed interest in. A few dials in, maybe a voicemail, and the contact rate is running cold. They conclude aged leads don't work.

That conclusion is usually wrong. What's usually wrong is the system — or the absence of one.

This is the operational guide for agents and small teams running aged insurance lead campaigns in 2026. Contact rate benchmarks, conversion math by sub-vertical, the cadence structure that actually produces contacts, and the TCPA compliance framing you need before you start dialing. No fluff.

## The four markets inside "aged insurance leads"

"Insurance" covers four distinct products with four different contact profiles, pricing ranges, and compliance postures. Treating them as one category is the first operational mistake.

**Auto insurance.** The cheapest aged inventory in the category — $0.25–$0.75 per record for 31–90 day leads. Contact rates are high (consumers are used to shopping auto frequently) but close rates are low. The economics work at volume. This is a cadence play, not a needs-based consultative sale.

**Life insurance (term).** $5–$15 per record. Higher per-lead cost is justified by higher commission structures. Consumers who shopped term life had a specific trigger event — marriage, new child, mortgage. If the trigger is still relevant, re-engagement is credible. Contact rate is lower than auto because the impulse was more considered.

**Final expense.** $0.62–$1.88 per record for internet form, $3–$8 for direct mail. Final expense aged leads are the highest-ROI aged lead product in the insurance category — the prospect demographics are stable (65+), the need doesn't expire, and the conversions per 1,000 contacts are consistently strong for agents who've built a working cadence. Saturday morning call windows produce disproportionate contact rates with this demographic.

**Health insurance (ACA, Medicare Advantage, Medicare Supplement).** $0.50–$0.94 for ACA aged, $1.50–$8 for Medicare. The CMS TPMO consent rule (effective December 2022) created a meaningful compliance distinction for Medicare leads — see the compliance section below. ACA aged leads have more flexibility, but OEP timing affects responsiveness.

For full pricing tables across all four sub-markets, see [Aged Lead Industry Statistics](/blog/aged-lead-industry-statistics). The pricing ranges above are Aged Lead Store's published rates — which is the most transparent pricing reference in the category.

## Contact rate benchmarks — what to actually expect

Contact rate on aged leads has a wider variance than most agents expect before they buy. Here are the benchmark ranges across the category, segmented by lead age:

| Lead age | Contact rate (optimized cadence) | Contact rate (no system) |
|---|---|---|
| 31–60 days | 20–28% | 8–12% |
| 61–90 days | 15–22% | 6–10% |
| 91–180 days | 10–16% | 3–7% |
| 180+ days | 6–10% | 1–4% |

The delta between "optimized cadence" and "no system" is the single biggest lever available to an aged-lead operator. It's not the lead quality. It's the calling window, the frequency structure, and the multi-channel coverage.

"Optimized" here means: 5–7 call attempts spread across different time windows over 10 days, with SMS and email layered in, using a re-engagement opener rather than a cold-call opener. The contact rate difference between dialing once at 10 AM on a Tuesday and running a 7-touch cadence across variable time windows is that 2–3x gap above.

A practical worked example. Agent A buys 500 aged auto insurance leads at $0.50 each — $250 total. No system: dials once per record at 9 AM, stops at 7%. That's 35 contacts. At a 15% quote rate, she gets 5 quotes. At 30% close, she closes 1.5 policies. Her cost per closing is $167.

Agent B buys the same 500 leads for the same $250. Runs a 7-touch cadence (described below), hits 22% contact rate — 110 contacts. Same 15% quote rate gives him 16 quotes. Same 30% close rate gives him 5 closings. His cost per closing is $50.

Same leads. Same budget. $167 vs $50 cost per closing. Volume changes everything, but the system matters first.

## The 5-part working system

### 1. Score before you dial

Not all records in a batch are created equal. Before you run your first call, sort your file:

- **High priority**: records with valid cell numbers (reverse-lookup confirmed), age 31–60 days, ZIP codes inside your licensed territory, lead type that matches your current product.
- **Medium priority**: records with landline indicators, age 61–90 days, or leads where the intent signal is less specific.
- **Low priority / suppress**: records with duplicate names across other batches, obvious bad-data indicators (555 numbers, clearly fake emails), ages above 90 days if you're already loaded.

You don't need a sophisticated AI scoring model to start (though see [AI Lead Scoring for Aged Lists](/blog/ai-scoring-aged-leads) if you want to go further). A simple spreadsheet sort on those criteria takes 20 minutes and reorders your working queue meaningfully.

### 2. Build a 7-touch cadence over 10 days

The AgedLeadStore guide gives you the skeleton. Here's the operator-level version:

**Day 1, 8:30 AM:** Call + voicemail. Voicemail should be ≤25 seconds. "Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] with [Agency]. You'd expressed interest in [auto/life/final expense] coverage a little while back — wanted to reach out and see if you're still exploring your options or if you found a good fit. I'll try you again, but you can also call me back at [number] anytime."

**Day 1, 5:30 PM:** SMS. "Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] from [Agency] — left you a voicemail earlier about your [coverage type] interest. Happy to answer questions by text if that's easier. No pressure." (Keep it human, not a blast template.)

**Day 3, 11:30 AM:** Call, no voicemail. If no answer, move on. Second voicemail on the same record in 3 days looks spammy.

**Day 3, email:** Short re-engagement email — "Subject: Still shopping [coverage type]?" Two sentences max. A question, not a pitch.

**Day 5, 4:30 PM:** Call + voicemail variant. Different opening: "Hi [Name], [Your Name] again — I know you've heard from me, and I don't want to be that agent. But if you're still in the market, I'd rather spend 5 minutes with you now than have you end up with a policy that doesn't fit. I'll leave you alone after this if it's not a good time."

**Day 7, SMS:** A different angle — send something helpful, not a re-ask. A rate comparison note, a quick stat about [vertical]-specific coverage changes in their state. Show value, not desperation.

**Day 10:** Final call. Clean disposition. If no contact after 7 touches, mark "exhausted" and move to a 30-day drip email sequence.

The call windows that produce the highest contact rates on insurance aged leads: 8–10 AM local time (catch before the day starts) and 4:30–7 PM (after work hours). Final expense specifically: Saturday 9–11 AM consistently outperforms weekday windows for the 65+ demographic.

### 3. Script the re-engagement opener, not the cold opener

The most expensive mistake in aged lead outreach is using a cold-call script. The person asked a question — they're not a stranger. Your opener should acknowledge that:

"You'd looked into [coverage type] a while back" → acknowledges they initiated
"I just wanted to follow up to see if you found what you needed" → helpful framing, not a pitch
"If you're still exploring, I'd love to take 5 minutes with you" → low-commitment ask

What to avoid: "Hi, I'm calling about life insurance — is this a good time?" That's a cold call. The record isn't cold. Your opener is cold.

Once you get someone on the line, the transition to needs discovery is standard insurance sales. The aged-lead-specific work is the re-engagement opener that gets them talking.

### 4. Layer channels without spamming

Insurance buyers in 2026 expect to hear from agents across multiple channels — but there's a compliance line between a multi-channel cadence and harassment. The working balance is: 2–3 call attempts per week maximum on a given record, 1 SMS per week (never two SMS on the same day as a call), 1 email per week during active cadence.

After 10 days without contact, drop to a 30-day email drip only. No calls, no SMS. This protects your compliance posture and your sender reputation.

### 5. Track every disposition, submit returns on time

The two numbers that determine whether aged leads work for your operation:

**Contact rate**: calls connected ÷ total records attempted. Track this at the batch level, not the total.

**Effective cost per lead**: [(total spend) - (returns received)] ÷ (total records net of returns). If you're buying at $0.50/record and submitting 15% returns at 20% return credit, your effective cost is $0.50 × (1 - 0.15 × 0.20) = $0.485. Not huge, but it compounds.

Aged Lead Store's return policy allows up to 20% credit for verifiable bad data, within 14 days. Submit returns systematically. Agents who skip the returns process and agents who track it religiously end up with materially different economics.

## TCPA-safe outreach in 2026 — what you actually need to know

The regulatory picture for aged insurance leads shifted significantly in 2025. Here's the current state.

**The FCC 1:1 consent rule: vacated and repealed.** The FCC's one-to-one consent requirement — which would have required individual lead consent to each specific entity contacting the consumer — was vacated by the Eleventh Circuit in January 2025 and formally repealed by the FCC in August 2025. The practical effect: the prior consent framework (a consumer agreeing to receive calls from "insurance providers" or similar broad categories on a lead form) is once again operative at the federal level for TCPA purposes.

This is significant context for anyone who bought compliance software or adjusted their cadence in 2024 in anticipation of the 1:1 rule. If you dialed back your outreach, you can re-evaluate.

**The Mode framework: three operating tiers.** Not all aged insurance lead outreach operates at the same compliance risk. The Operator's System — our flagship playbook — frames this as Mode A, Mode B, and Mode C:

**Mode A (Conservative):** Manual dialing only. No auto-dialers. No predictive dialers. Verifiable DNC scrub within 30 days. TCPA-documented consent chain in the lead record. Reserved for compliance-conscious operations or states with elevated state-level TCPA exposure. This is the lowest-risk operating mode.

**Mode B (Operator Standard):** The middle of the practical range for most working operators. Manual or click-to-dial (not predictive). DNC scrub within 30 days. Consent chain documented but not necessarily individually verified for each record. This is where most solo agents and small teams operate in 2026.

**Mode C (Not Recommended):** Predictive or auto-dialing without verified individual consent. Even with the FCC 1:1 rule vacated, predictive dialing on aged lists without confirmed consent creates meaningful exposure — especially in states with their own mini-TCPA statutes. The Operator's System includes Mode C for completeness, not as a recommendation.

For formal legal guidance on your specific operation, [Henson Legal](https://www.henson-legal.com/) is the TCPA counsel we recommend.

**State mini-TCPAs: where the risk actually lives in 2026.** Federal TCPA isn't your biggest exposure in most states today — state-level statutes are. Key ones affecting insurance outreach:

- **Florida (FTSA):** The Florida Telephone Solicitation Act has a private right of action and $500/call damages. Requires prior express written consent for any auto-dialed call or text. Florida agents buying aged lists should verify consent documentation for any FL records.
- **Oklahoma (OTSA):** The Oklahoma Telephone Solicitation Act is similar in structure to FTSA. Private right of action, per-call damages. Verify consent chain on OK records.
- **Washington (HB 1497):** Enacted in 2025. Expands consent requirements for automated and pre-recorded calls. WA contacts in aged lists require additional verification.
- **Maryland and Texas:** Both have had enhanced mini-TCPA enforcement through 2025–2026. Not as aggressive as FL/OK/WA but worth documented DNC scrubbing.

The practical implication: if you're buying aged insurance leads with national coverage, segment your contact list by state and apply Mode A protocols to FL, OK, WA, MD, and TX records. The other 45 states can run Mode B. This isn't a compliance burden — it's a 10-minute segmentation step in your CRM.

**The revocation-all provision.** Federal TCPA revocation-of-consent rules were originally set to allow consumers to revoke consent for all communications simultaneously (not just one channel). This provision has been delayed to January 2027. Practically, this means a consumer asking you to stop calling is a revocation of calling, not necessarily of email — but document it either way.

## Where to buy and how to filter

The quality of your aged insurance leads is largely determined by how you filter at purchase. The variables that matter most:

**Lead age bracket.** For a new operation, start with 31–90 day inventory. The contact rates are highest and you'll calibrate your cadence against a responsive baseline before moving into 90–180 day records.

**Geography.** Buy within your licensed territories. This sounds obvious but buyers from multi-state agencies sometimes pull records they can't legally sell in. Filtered state-by-state coverage also lets you apply the state-level compliance segmentation described above.

**Exclusivity and data quality.** Internet-form aged leads are typically non-exclusive (the consumer submitted to a lead form, which may have been distributed to multiple buyers). That's priced in. Factor an average 10–15% bad data rate into your return economics before your first purchase.
