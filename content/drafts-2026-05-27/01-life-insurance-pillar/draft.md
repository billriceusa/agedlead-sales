# Aged Life Insurance Leads: The 2026 Agent's Guide to Pricing, Cadence, and ROI

Every life insurance agent working non-referral acquisition asks the same question eventually: why am I paying $30-50 per real-time lead when most of them aren't answering the phone either?

That's the right question. Aged internet-form life insurance leads — the same consumer data, 30-180 days old — run $0.62 to $15 depending on the product type and lead age. The original buyers moved on after one or two dials. You get the same record for 80-90% less.

The nuance most agents miss: "aged life insurance leads" isn't a single category. Final expense, term, mortgage protection, and IUL all age at different rates, convert at different rates, and require different approach cadences. Working a 90-day IUL prospect the same way you'd work a 30-day final expense inquiry is the fastest way to underperform a channel that actually works.

This is the complete picture: what aged life insurance leads are, how the economics differ by product line, what a proper cadence looks like in 2026, and why the compliance environment is more favorable today than it's been in three years.

## What aged life insurance leads are

An aged life insurance lead is a consumer who filled out an internet form requesting a quote — term coverage, final expense, an IUL illustration, or mortgage protection — that was sold to its initial buyers within minutes of submission, then released into the aged marketplace after going unworked.

"Released" is the operative word. It's not a dead lead. It's a lead whose original buyers — typically three to five different agents — made one or two call attempts and stopped. The consumer still has the same coverage gap. Their mortgage didn't disappear. Their concern about final expense coverage for an aging parent didn't resolve itself. They just stopped hearing from agents.

You get that record at a fraction of the original cost because the premium-for-freshness has expired. The data hasn't changed. The competition for it has nearly disappeared.

## Four product lines, four different aging curves

This is where most batch-and-blast approaches break down. Aged life leads are not homogeneous inventory.

**Final expense** leads age quickly in terms of urgency but slowly in terms of relevance. The need — affordable burial coverage or end-of-life protection, typically for a senior parent — doesn't go away with time. What does fade is the immediate anxiety that prompted the form fill. A 90-day final expense lead is still in-market; the prospect just needs a different re-engagement frame than a fresh inquiry. Lead with the coverage gap, not the fact that they filled out a form.

**Term life** leads are moderately perishable. Most term buyers are in a defined life moment: new mortgage, new child, small business launch. If you're calling a 120-day-old term lead, the triggering event may have passed — or the prospect may have already bought. Contact rates on term aged leads drop meaningfully past 90 days. The productive window is 8-85 days.

**IUL** leads behave differently than anything else in the category. These are conversations about tax-advantaged wealth accumulation, retirement income, or estate planning — not about affordability or urgency. A well-qualified IUL prospect at day 180 is still worth contacting because the original decision cycle was long to begin with. More important: the commission math changes what's affordable. A single IUL close at $5,000-10,000 in first-year compensation justifies a lead cost an order of magnitude above what final expense economics support.

**Mortgage protection** leads are tightly bound to a triggering event: a recent home purchase or refinance. These decay fastest. A 90-day-old mortgage protection lead is two to three months past the transaction that generated the original inquiry. Buy these in the 8-30 day tier or not at all.

## The math — two agents, same $1,500 budget

Agent A buys real-time final expense internet leads at $35 each. $1,500 budget: 42 leads. At a 40% contact rate — and remember, those three to five original buyers are calling the same list simultaneously, so effective contact for any single buyer is lower — she reaches about 17 prospects in a meaningful conversation. At 8% close on conversations: 1.4 policies. Call it one to two closed per campaign. Average FYC: $800. Revenue: $800–$1,600. Cost per issued policy: $750–$1,500.

Agent B buys 31-85 day aged final expense internet leads at $1.25 each. Same $1,500 budget: 1,200 leads. He runs a 14-day cadence — Day 1 call + voicemail, Day 2 email, Day 3 SMS, Day 5 call, Day 7 email, Day 10 call, Day 12 email, Day 14 final call. Cumulative contact rate: 22%. That's 264 conversations. At 5% close on conversations: 13 policies. Average FYC: $800. Revenue: $10,400. Cost per issued policy: $115.

Read those numbers again.

Same budget. Same product line. Same closing skill. Agent A gets one to two policies. Agent B gets 13.

The close rate on aged prospect conversations is lower — 5% versus 8% — because these prospects require more re-engagement framing. That's the tradeoff. The math absorbs it and then some: cost per issued policy is 6-13x more efficient on aged. The reason is volume. Agent B is having 15x more conversations on the same spend.

"Volume changes everything" isn't a cliché in this context. It's the entire business model.

For IUL, the math looks different but the logic amplifies. At $4 per aged 31-85 day IUL lead and a $1,500 budget, you're buying 375 records. Expect a 15-18% contact rate and a longer close cycle. But a single IUL close at $7,500 FYC against a $1,500 lead spend is a 5:1 return before you factor in ongoing trail commissions. The cost-per-closing tolerance is simply higher.

## Cadence — what changes by product type

All aged lead cadences follow the same structural logic: 8 touches across 14 days, decreasing urgency in tone as the sequence progresses. But the opener, value proposition, and channel weight should match the product.

**Final expense**: Open with the coverage gap, not the form fill. *"I saw you were researching final expense coverage — most families looking at this are protecting a parent or a spouse from unexpected burial costs. That's what I specialize in. I'd like to spend five minutes with you."* Dial heavy in the first seven days. This buyer responds to accessibility and warmth, not sophistication. Email subject lines: *"Coverage question"* and *"Re: final expense inquiry."*

**Term life**: The life moment is the hook. *"You requested term quotes — most people doing that are protecting something new: a home, a family, a business. Whatever changed for you, I can help you compare options that fit your timeline."* Email focus: the gap between what they have and what they need, with worked examples specific to common scenarios (30-year term for a 38-year-old with a 25-year mortgage).

**IUL**: Don't open with the product name. Open with the goal: *"You looked into strategies to build tax-advantaged retirement income — I specialize in that space and want to share a few illustrations."* Expect lower contact rates (15-18%) and a longer close cycle: plan two to three conversations before commitment. The cadence can stretch to 21 days. Commission math justifies the patience.

**Mortgage protection**: Speed matters more here than for any other aged life product. A 30-day mortgage protection lead is still relatively fresh relative to the original transaction. Work these on a 7-day compressed cadence, lean on the home purchase hook, and sunset the campaign at day 14 rather than 21.

## The 2026 compliance picture

Aged internet-form life insurance leads carry the original consumer's TCPA consent from the time they completed the quote request form. That consent is still valid under federal law — the FCC's 1:1 consent rule, which would have required per-seller written consent on each lead-gen form, was vacated by the 11th Circuit in January 2025 and formally repealed by the FCC in August 2025. One-to-many disclosed consent remains permissible at the federal level.

What didn't change: revocation applies, and the window is shorter. As of April 2025, consumers can revoke consent via "any reasonable means" — a text, a verbal request on a call — and you must honor it within 10 business days. Your CRM needs to receive and process opt-outs in real time. This is not a gray area.

State-level exposure: if you're operating in Florida, Oklahoma, Washington, or Texas, state mini-TCPA statutes (FL FTSA, OK OTSA, WA HB 1497, TX) can impose stricter requirements than federal law. Verify consent language on any leads purchased in those states and scrub against your own opt-out records before every campaign.

On AI voice: if your prospecting system uses AI-generated voice, the FCC's February 2024 ruling classifies that as "artificial voice" under TCPA §227(b)(1) — prior express written consent is required. Most aged-lead callers are using human or pre-recorded-human voice systems, so this is only relevant if you've moved to an AI voice agent.

The conservative approach — call Mode A: buy only leads with verifiable documented consent, scrub against the National DNC before every campaign, and honor opt-outs within 10 business days. ## Getting started — what to do Monday

**Buy in volume.** The math only works at scale. A $300 test buy of 240 aged final expense leads at $1.25 each is a valid first experiment. A $300 test buy of eight real-time leads at $37 is not. The law of averages requires surface area.

**Set up the cadence before the first dial.** Import your records into your CRM, queue the 8-touch sequence, load email and SMS templates. Running a batch without a system means you're only reaching 20-25% of the contacts you paid for. The first call is a small fraction of your eventual conversations.

**Segment by product type and age before dialing.** Final expense leads go in one queue with a final expense opener. IUL leads go in another. Don't mix your 30-day term leads with your 90-day mortgage protection leads. The scripts, the value proposition, and the expectation on close timeline are all different.

**Track cost per issued policy.** Not cost per lead. Those numbers differ by a factor of 30-50x when you compare aged and real-time. If you're only watching cost per lead, you'll misread the economics of every campaign you run.
