# Aged Life Insurance Leads: The 2026 Guide for Term, Final Expense, and IUL Agents

Most agents come to aged leads the same way: real-time costs got too high, conversion rates got frustrating, and someone in a forum said "try aged." That's usually where the confusion starts — because "aged life insurance leads" isn't one product. It's three, and the economics of each are different enough that buying the wrong sub-market wastes a month of budget before you figure out what went wrong.

This is the full picture: what aged leads are in each life insurance sub-vertical, what they actually cost, how the ROI math works, and how to run a campaign that produces results in 60 days.

## What aged life insurance leads actually are

An aged life insurance lead is a consumer who submitted an internet form requesting a quote — term coverage, a final expense policy, an IUL illustration — and whose record was sold to its original real-time buyers within minutes of form completion. Those buyers had anywhere from 5 to 30 days to work the lead before it was released into the aged marketplace.

By the time you're buying at day 30, day 60, or day 180, you're working the same data: name, phone, email, coverage amount requested, smoking/tobacco status, date of birth. What changed is the price — and the competition. The original three to five buyers have moved on. You're not racing anyone.

That's the whole business case: identical data, dramatically lower cost, no speed-to-lead arms race.

## Three sub-markets, three different economics

Life insurance is the most segmented lead category in the industry. Which sub-market you're in determines how much to pay, how long to work each record, which script approach to use, and what CPA is good versus mediocre.

**Term life.** Mid-market product. Monthly premiums run $20–$150 for healthy applicants; first-year commissions run $400–$2,000 depending on face amount and carrier. High-volume model — agents who succeed in term write dozens of policies a month, not a few. Aged term is the most competitive sub-market in life insurance, which means the best inventory moves fast and the rest requires a disciplined cadence to convert.

**Final expense.** The senior market. Monthly premiums are $30–$80, first-year commissions $500–$1,500, and most carriers pay 110–120% target premium in year one on small whole-life policies. Final expense is the sub-vertical where aged leads consistently produce the best CPA — because the underlying need doesn't expire on a 90-day clock. A 70-year-old who inquired about burial coverage six months ago is still 70 years old with a mortality concern.

**Indexed universal life (IUL).** The high-net-worth segment. Monthly premiums start at $300 and routinely run past $1,000. First-year commissions are $3,000–$10,000+ on a single sale. The sales cycle is multi-touch and multi-week, often involving illustrations and a written proposal. Competition on aged IUL records is effectively zero — most agents don't know aged IUL inventory exists.

Same industry. Three completely different buying models.

## The ROI math — run it sub-vertical by sub-vertical

Sticker price isn't the useful number. Cost per acquisition is. Here's what the math looks like across all three sub-markets at a $2,500 monthly budget.

**Term life — the volume play with a long tail**

Budget: $2,500. Lead cost: $8 (aged 31–85 day, shared). Leads purchased: 312. On a 14-day, six-touch cadence, a realistic cumulative contact rate is 22% — that's 69 conversations. Term-to-close conversion on aged records runs 2%–3% without real-time competition. Call it 1.8 closings. At a $1,000 average FYC: $1,800 revenue. Net of $2,500 lead spend: –$700 in month one.

That's a loss. But that 30-day snapshot is the wrong measurement for aged term. Agents who run a 90-day follow-up cadence on the same batch routinely see effective conversion double over the extended window. The stronger use case for aged term: the gateway into multi-product sales. Write the term policy, build the relationship, cross-sell IUL or whole life within 12 months. The term closing subsidized your cost to acquire a long-term client.

**Final expense — where aged leads are dominant**

Budget: $2,500. Lead cost: $1.25 (aged 31–85 day, internet form, shared). Leads purchased: 2,000. That's not a typo. Contact rate over a 21-day cadence: 20%–25% = 450 conversations. FE conversion on aged records for an experienced telesales agent runs 1%–2% on contacts. Call it 5.6 closings. At a $700 average FYC: $3,920 revenue. Net of $2,500 lead spend: +$1,420 in month one.

The math works because the sticker price is so low ($1.25) that volume covers the conversion gap entirely. You don't need a high close rate to be profitable. You need a high enough contact rate and a consistent cadence.

Direct mail response FE is a separate tier worth understanding. At $15/lead (aged exclusive), $2,500 buys 167 records. Conversion on direct-mail-response FE runs 6%–8% — the consumer took an offline action to request the quote, so the signal is much stronger than an internet form. At 7% conversion: 11.7 closings × $700 FYC = $8,190 revenue. Net of $2,500 lead spend: $5,690. Higher gross revenue from the same budget, lower call volume. Both models work — which one you run depends on your call capacity.

**IUL — highest leverage, lowest competition**

Budget: $2,500. Lead cost: $4 (aged 31–85 day, shared). Leads purchased: 625. Contact rate: 20% = 125 conversations. IUL is a consultative, multi-touch sale — conversion on a 30-day cadence runs 0.5%–1% on contacts. Call it 0.75 closings. At $5,000 FYC: $3,750 revenue. Net of $2,500 lead spend: +$1,250.

That looks modest in month one. What makes aged IUL compelling is the 90-to-180-day pipeline view. An agent who buys 625 IUL records in May and runs a consistent follow-up cadence through October may close 4–6 sales from the same batch. The 6-month CPA looks dramatically different from the 30-day CPA.

And competition on aged IUL is virtually zero. If you sell IUL and you're not buying aged, you're overpaying for real-time leads and competing with agents who are.

## The aging curve is different in life insurance

This is the non-obvious insight most agents miss when they first test aged life leads.

In auto insurance, a 180-day-old prospect may have already renewed — the problem is temporarily solved. In mortgage purchase, a 180-day-old lead may have already closed on a home. In those verticals, conversion rates decay sharply as records age.

Life insurance is structurally different. A 180-day-old final expense lead is still 70+ years old. The mortality concern that drove the original inquiry hasn't resolved. A 180-day-old IUL lead is still building retirement wealth. A 180-day-old term lead still has a mortgage and kids.

The implication: aged life insurance leads hold conversion value at older record ages better than almost any other vertical. The 86–180 day cohort — the cheapest tier in the aged life market — often outperforms on cost-per-closing because the price drops so dramatically at older ages while the underlying need stays constant. Most agents overbuy fresh inventory (31–85 days) and ignore the 86–180 day tier. If you run FE or IUL, that tier deserves a real test with real budget.

## How to run a 21-day aged life insurance lead campaign

Where most agents fail isn't the lead quality. It's cadence.

One call isn't a campaign. Aged leads require six touches minimum to extract the full conversion value from a batch. For term and FE: two phone calls, two texts, one voicemail drop, one email — spread over 21 days. For IUL: a longer 30-day sequence, heavier on email and multi-attempt phone, lighter on text (the HNW segment responds differently to text outreach than the FE demographic). The [Outreach Cadence Calculator](/calculators/outreach-cadence) will model the exact touch sequence for your volume and call capacity.

Script adjustment is the second variable agents consistently get wrong. The aged pitch is not the real-time pitch. You're not competing with anyone — that changes the opening completely. "You filled out a quote request back in [month] — I know it's been a while. Rates have shifted since then and I wanted to run a fresh look for you. Do you have two minutes?" That script works better at day 90 than "I see you just requested a quote online."

Lead management by cohort: import your 30-day records first, then 60-day, then 90-day. Don't run them on the same daily list. Track contact rate, conversation rate, and close rate separately by cohort. Within 60 days you'll have clear data on which cohort produces the best CPA in your specific market — and the numbers will tell you where to put the rest of your budget.

## The 2026 compliance frame for aged life insurance leads

Two things every aged life agent needs to have right in 2026.

**Original consent documentation.** Aged internet-form leads carry the consumer's original TCPA consent from the form-fill. Get the documentation from your provider — the exact disclosure language, the timestamp, the originating URL. Reputable aged-lead vendors provide this on request. If a vendor can't produce it, walk away.

**Senior-demographic compliance.** Final expense is the sub-vertical with the highest compliance exposure because the demographic is 60+. Florida's FTSA, Oklahoma's OTSA, and Washington's HB 1497 impose tighter restrictions on calls to seniors than the federal TCPA. If you're calling into Florida or Oklahoma with FE records, layer in the state rules before you dial a single number.

The FCC's 1:1 consent rule was vacated by the 11th Circuit in January 2025 and formally repealed in August 2025 — good news for the aged market, because broader disclosed consent from original form fills still holds. The revocation rule is fully active: consumers may revoke consent through any reasonable means and must be honored within 10 business days. Your CRM needs to track that in real time, across all campaigns, not just the one where the STOP was received.
