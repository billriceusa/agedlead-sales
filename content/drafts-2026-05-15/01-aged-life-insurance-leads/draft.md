# Aged Life Insurance Leads: The 2026 Agent's Guide to Buying and Working Them

The agent who buys aged final expense leads at $1.25 per record and the agent who buys real-time at $25 per record are both trying to reach the same consumer. One bought access to that consumer at 8 a.m. on the day the form came in. The other bought the same record 45 days later, when the real-time buyers had already given up and the lead moved into the aged marketplace.

That $23.75 cost gap isn't theoretical. At $1,500 a month, it's 60 leads versus 1,200 leads. That's where the math on aged life insurance leads gets interesting — and where agents who are still buying real-time exclusively are leaving significant net revenue on the table.

But there isn't one aged life insurance lead market. There are three — term, final expense, and indexed universal life — and each one has a different case for aged versus real-time, a different pricing range, and a different working cadence. The agent who buys all three the same way is almost certainly optimizing incorrectly for at least one of them.

Here's the full picture.

## Why the sub-market you choose matters as much as the age tier

**Final expense** is the clearest case for aged leads. The consumer — typically 50–80 years old, looking for $5,000–$25,000 of whole-life coverage to cover burial expenses — doesn't stop needing burial insurance just because an agent called last month. Financial need in this market is persistent. A 90-day-old FE lead is still highly workable. Many experienced final expense agents build their entire pipeline on aged inventory and never touch real-time at current prices.

**Term life** is a mixed case. Term leads often represent a specific life event — a new mortgage, a new baby, a recent job change — and that urgency can decay. A consumer shopping for $500,000 of 20-year term in January who doesn't buy may still be uninsured in April, or they may have bought through another agent. Aged term leads work, but the conversion rate curve drops faster than final expense. You need volume and a crisp call opener that re-establishes the original intent.

**IUL** is the most different. An indexed universal life policy is a multi-step consultative sale — illustrations, retirement income projections, often multiple conversations over weeks. The aged-lead case for IUL is real (the commission math justifies a longer pipeline), but it's a nurture play, not a dial-and-close play. A consumer who expressed interest in tax-advantaged retirement income 60 days ago is still a qualified prospect — but they need an education sequence, not a closing pitch on the first call.

Knowing which sub-market you're in determines what a good result looks like, which cadence to run, and how much you should expect to pay per policy sold.

## What aged life insurance leads actually are

An aged life insurance lead is an internet-form submission — a consumer who requested a quote on a comparison site, insurer landing page, or financial-planning platform — that was sold to real-time buyers first, then released into the aged marketplace between 8 days and 24 months later.

The origination chain is standard across the industry: consumer submits form → quote engine sells the record to 2–5 real-time buyers simultaneously → those buyers call within minutes → some close, most don't → the unclosed record transfers to the aged inventory marketplace after a holding period, typically 7–14 days post-origination.

The data hasn't changed when you receive it. Name, phone number(s), email, address, date of birth, coverage interest (term amount, burial coverage amount, or retirement/IUL interest), and in many cases health classification (tobacco use, height and weight, general health tier). What changed is the freshness premium and the competition. By day 45, the original buyers have moved on. The lead is yours to work without three other agents calling the same prospect.

**Age tiers you'll encounter in 2026:**

- **8–30 days:** Early aged. Freshest inventory, still some competition from original buyers in active markets. Best for agents who want something between real-time pricing and real-time volume.
- **31–85 days:** The core of the aged market. Original buyers have given up. Best combination of volume, pricing, and workability.
- **86–365 days:** Deep aged. Heavily discounted, requires volume to work consistently. Final expense agents use this tier widely; harder for term.
- **366+ days:** Ultra-aged. Near-zero cost. Final expense and some term agents use these for warm-list supplementation or as training inventory for new dialers.

## The math: two final expense agents, same $1,500

Same vertical. Same product. Same 14-day working window. Different lead source.

**Agent A — buys real-time final expense internet forms**

Budget: $1,500. Lead cost: $25 each. Leads purchased: 60. Shared leads — also sold to 2–4 other agents calling the same window. Contact rate with power dialer in first 48 hours: 25% = 15 conversations. But 25–30% of those conversations are dismissive repeats ("I already got three calls about this"). Effective conversion rate on productive conversations: 8% = 1.2 policies. At $1,200 average first-year commission: $1,440 gross. Net after lead spend: –$60.

**Agent B — buys aged final expense internet forms (31–85 days)**

Budget: $1,500. Lead cost: $1.25 each. Leads purchased: 1,200. Cumulative contact rate over a 14-day, 6-touch cadence: 15% = 180 productive conversations. No same-week competition — the original buyers moved on weeks ago. Conversion rate on conversations: 4% = 7.2 policies. At $1,200 FYC: $8,640 gross. Net after lead spend: $7,140.

Read those numbers again. Same budget. Same product. Agent B is closing six times as many policies and netting $7,200 more per month from the same dollar spend. The lead cost advantage creates the volume. The volume is what lets the law of averages work.

The work is running a real 14-day cadence on 1,200 records — that's the ask. The cost arbitrage is the payoff.

## Working aged life leads: what changes by sub-market

### Final expense cadence (8–10 days)

FE is phone-first, short cycle. The consumer knows roughly what they want — they're looking for an agent who makes it easy to act on a decision they've already made.

- **Day 1:** Call + voicemail ("I'm following up on your request for burial coverage information..."). Email same day.
- **Day 3:** Second call, no voicemail. Text if mobile number is confirmed — one line, not a pitch ("Hi [name], wanted to see if you had questions on the coverage options I sent").
- **Day 5:** Third call with voicemail. New angle on the message — "wanted to check if the coverage amount you were looking at still fits your situation."
- **Day 7:** Email with one sentence of context: "I help families lock in a fixed monthly premium that doesn't increase, regardless of health changes down the road."
- **Day 9:** Final call. If no contact, flag for a 30-day re-work.

The FE conversation works on empathy and decisiveness. You're not introducing the need — most prospects have been thinking about this for months. You're becoming the agent who makes it easy to act.

### Term life cadence (12–14 days)

Longer, more email-heavy. The first call re-establishes context rather than assuming it: "You requested information on term coverage — are you still in a position where you're thinking about income protection for your family?" The term conversation is a comparison sale — the prospect is likely evaluating term versus whole life or versus doing nothing. Be ready with two scenarios and the monthly premium math.

The aging curve is faster on term than FE, so the opener has to do more re-qualification work. A rate-based hook helps: "With term rates where they are right now, I wanted to revisit the scenario you were looking at and see if the numbers still make sense."

### IUL cadence (21–30 days)

IUL is an education sale. You don't open by trying to close — you open by building understanding. The first two touches are informational: a brief email explaining what IUL does in plain language (index-linked growth, floor protection, flexible premiums, tax treatment). The third touch is a consultation offer — not a sales call. Fourth touch is the illustration request. A close typically doesn't happen before touch five or six.

Aged IUL leads in the 31–85 day range are prospects who expressed interest in something they likely didn't fully understand when they submitted the form. The conversation that wins is the one that meets them where their comprehension is now, not where a marketing form assumed they were. ## Compliance in 2026: what you need to know before you dial

Aged internet-form life insurance leads carry the original TCPA consent from when the consumer submitted the quote request. The FCC's 1:1 consent rule — which would have required individual, per-advertiser consent on each lead form — was vacated by the 11th Circuit in January 2025 and formally repealed by the FCC in August 2025. The federal standard has reverted to one-to-many disclosed consent: a lead form that says "you may be contacted by insurance agents" is generally sufficient.

Three things still require attention regardless:

1. **Revocation.** A consumer who says "don't call me" — on any channel, in any call — has revoked consent under the FCC's April 2025 rule. You have 10 business days to honor that revocation, and it applies to all marketing communications, not just the channel they used to revoke.

2. **AI voice.** If you're using an AI voice dialer (Synthflow, Air.ai, Retell, or similar), that call requires prior express written consent under the FCC's February 2024 "artificial voice" ruling. A generic lead form consent doesn't satisfy this.

3. **State mini-TCPAs.** FL (FTSA), OK (OTSA), WA (HB 1497), MD, and TX each have state-level statutes that can be more restrictive than the federal standard. Verify your exposure in those states before running a multi-state dialing campaign.

The full compliance framework is in [TCPA compliance for calling aged leads](/blog/tcpa-compliance-calling-aged-leads). ## What to look for in an aged life insurance lead vendor

Quality variance in the aged life lead market is real. Five things separate a reliable vendor from a time-sink:

**Data hygiene.** How recently were the phone numbers re-verified? How many numbers per record? A 45-day-old FE lead with three validated numbers is operationally different from the same lead with a single number last checked at origination.

**Filter depth.** For final expense specifically, you need age-range filtering — the sweet spot is 50–75, and a batch heavy on 78-year-olds has different underwriting implications. IUL leads should filter by income or coverage interest if the vendor supports it.

**Age granularity.** Can you buy 31–45 day leads separately from 60–85 day leads? The conversion curve inside a single age bracket isn't flat. Tiered purchasing lets you test and optimize.

**Return policy.** Industry standard is a 10–20% return cap for disconnected or wrong numbers. A vendor offering no returns on aged inventory is a flag.

**TCPA documentation.** Before scaling, confirm where the leads originated and what the consent language was on the originating form. Vendors who can't answer that question are selling something you can't use confidently at volume.

## Start here

If you're building an aged life insurance lead campaign for the first time:

- Pick one sub-market. Final expense has the most consistent math as a starting point.
- Budget $750–$1,500 for your first batch. At $1.25 per record, that's 600–1,200 leads — enough sample to let the law of averages do its work.
- Import into your CRM within 24 hours. A 1,200-record batch that sits for a week is 1,200 reasons your first test will underperform.
- Run at least 5 touches over 10–14 days. Anything less and you're testing follow-through, not the leads.
- Track cost per conversation and cost per policy. Contact rate is a leading indicator. Cost per closing is the only number that decides whether to scale.
- After 30 days, you'll have enough data to know whether your close rate per conversation justifies the investment.

The economics are there. The variable is execution.
