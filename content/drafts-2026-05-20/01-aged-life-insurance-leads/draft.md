# Aged Life Insurance Leads: The 2026 Operator's Guide (Term, FE, and IUL)

Life insurance agents ask one question about aged leads, but they're actually asking three. Aged term leads, aged final expense leads, and aged IUL leads are three different products with three different pricing tiers, three different sales cycles, and three different answers to whether the economics make sense for your operation.

Most content in this category treats "aged life insurance leads" as a single category. It isn't. The agent buying $1.25 final expense records to run a hundred-call week and the producer buying $5 IUL records to run a consultative multi-week pipeline are doing fundamentally different work. Conflating them is why so many agents try aged life, make the wrong purchase decision for their sub-market, and walk away with the wrong conclusion.

This guide is the full picture: what each sub-category actually costs, why aged life insurance leads age better than almost any other vertical, the specific cadence and economics for each, and how to buy correctly in 2026.

## Why life insurance leads age differently than other verticals

This is the insight most articles skip, so let's put it first.

In auto insurance, a 180-day-old lead may have already renewed their policy. The problem that drove the original form-fill may be solved. In short-term mortgage purchase, the borrower may have already bought a home, stopped looking, or shifted to a rental. The intent decays sharply past 60–90 days.

Life insurance is different. A 180-day-old final expense lead is still 70+ years old. The mortality concern that drove the original inquiry hasn't resolved itself. A 180-day-old IUL lead is still building wealth toward retirement. A 180-day-old term lead is still a parent with a mortgage and dependents.

The underlying need doesn't have an expiration date. That structural difference means aged life leads hold their conversion potential at older ages better than aged leads in any other vertical — which changes the math at every age bracket.

Most agents over-buy the 30–85 day tier because it "feels" more like a warm lead. The 86–180 day cohort at $0.50–$2 per record is one of the most under-bought segments in the entire life insurance category. If you're not testing it, you're paying a freshness premium for a distinction the prospect doesn't share.

## The three sub-verticals — what you're actually buying

Before the pricing table, the context that makes sense of it.

**Term life.** The mid-market product. Monthly premiums run $20–$150 for healthy applicants, first-year commissions run $400–$2,000 depending on face amount and carrier. Term is a volume model — agents who make it work write hundreds of policies per year, not dozens. The typical term buyer is 25–45, has a mortgage or young children, and shops on price. Aged term leads run $5–$15 for 31–85 day records.

**Final expense.** The senior market — burial insurance, usually whole-life policies with $10K–$25K face amounts, monthly premiums of $30–$80. First-year commissions run $500–$1,500, often at 110–120% target premium on small whole-life. Most final expense telesales agents run 20–30 dials a day. Aged internet-form FE leads run $0.62–$1.88. Aged direct mail response leads — a different product entirely — run $8–$25 for exclusives.

**Indexed universal life (IUL).** The high-net-worth segment. Monthly premiums start at $300 and run past $1,000 routinely. First-year commissions are $3,000–$10,000+ on a single sale. The sales cycle is multi-touch, multi-week — illustrations, retirement income conversations, written proposals. Aged IUL leads run $2–$6 per record.

Three different consumers. Three different sales cycles. Three different commission structures. The pricing reflects all of it.

## Term life: the volume model and where aged fits

Term life is the hardest of the three sub-markets to build a strong economic case for aged leads — and it still works.

At a $40 shared real-time term lead with 5% effective conversion (factoring in the typical four-to-eight buyer competition on real-time term auctions), cost per acquisition is $800. Average term FYC of $800–$1,200 on a $500K 20-year policy for a healthy 35-year-old: gross margin per sale is $0–$400. Functional, barely. One bad conversion month and the unit economics invert.

At $8 aged term, 1.5% conversion on contacts: $533 CPA. Better than real-time — but the margin between them is narrower than in the other two sub-markets.

The honest caveat on aged term: conversion decay is steeper than in FE or IUL. Term buyers are commodity rate-shoppers. By 30–90 days post-form-fill, many have already bought or moved to a different product. The 31–85 day tier still produces, but the 86–180 day tier drops off more steeply in term than it does in the other sub-verticals.

Where aged term life insurance leads make the most sense: as an entry product for a multi-product agency. Sell the term policy, then convert the relationship to IUL, whole life, or FE over time. The economics of aged term get substantially better when you credit back the value of that long-term relationship rather than calculating the term CPA in isolation.

Recommended cadence for aged term: 21 days, 8 touches. Initial contact on day 1, follow-up days 2, 4, 7, 10, 14, 18, 21. Mix of calls and texts. Script leads with the rate-shopping angle — acknowledge that they shopped, offer a fast comparison.

## Final expense: where the math gets quiet and profitable

Final expense is where aged leads produce the most consistent, predictable economics in the entire life insurance vertical — if you match the right lead type to the right operational model.

The direct mail response tier is the outlier. A direct mail response FE lead — a record where the consumer responded to a mailed offer by returning a card or calling a number — costs $8–$25 aged exclusive. That's 10–20x more than an aged internet-form FE record. The premium exists because the consumer took an offline action. They're harder to fake, less likely to be in active rate-shop mode, and they convert at substantially higher rates.

Let me show you the CPA math for both.

**Aged DM response FE.** $25/lead at 8% conversion rate (realistic for an experienced FE telesales agent running a good script): CPA = $313. Average FE FYC of $700: gross margin per sale = $387.

**Aged internet-form FE.** $1.25/lead at 1% conversion rate (realistic cold): CPA = $125. Same $700 FYC: gross margin per sale = $575.

Both are profitable. They're different operations. The DM aged model is 25 dials a day, 2–3 conversations, 1 close every 1–2 days. The internet-form aged model is 60–80 dials a day, 4–6 conversations, 1 close every 2–3 days. Neither one is superior — they fit different agents.

The case for internet-form aged FE at volume: $1.25 per record against $125 CPA is among the most forgiving economics in any lead vertical. A well-managed agency can sustain that CPA at full-cycle scale, including bad-data returns, re-dials, and bad months.

One compliance note specific to final expense: the senior demographic (60+) carries a higher TCPA risk profile than other life sub-markets. Florida's FTSA, Oklahoma's OTSA, and Washington's HB 1497 all impose tighter restrictions on outbound calls to seniors than the federal TCPA floor. If you're running a high-volume FE telesales operation, mode of contact and consent basis matter more here than in term or IUL. The [Aged Lead Operator's System](/playbook/insurance) covers this directly, including the Mode A / Mode B calling framework for aged consent documentation.

Recommended cadence for aged FE: 21 days, 10 touches. Senior demographics respond to persistence but not pressure. Lead with benefit, not urgency.

## IUL: where the math becomes impossible to ignore

IUL is the sub-market where aged leads become almost mathematically unassailable for independent producers.

The commission ceiling is high enough to absorb virtually any cost structure. A single IUL policy at $500/month in premium produces $5,000–$8,000 in first-year commission. One closing can fund months of lead spend. That changes the ROI arithmetic entirely.

Now layer the aged-to-real-time spread on IUL: real-time runs $40–$75. Aged runs $2–$6. That's a 10–20x price gap on a product where the average sale produces $5,000+ in commission.

Run the math on both.

**Agent A — aged IUL.** $5,000 budget at $5/lead = 1,000 records. 25% cumulative contact rate over 30-day multi-touch cadence = 250 conversations. 1% conversion to bound IUL on contacts (long cycle, consultative close) = 2.5 sales. At $5,000 average FYC: revenue $12,500. Net after lead spend: $7,500. CPA: $2,000.

**Agent B — real-time IUL.** $5,000 budget at $50/lead = 100 records. High contact rate at first touch (speed-to-lead), but competing with 2–4 other producers on every record. Effective conversion at 3% on contacts = 3 sales. Revenue: $15,000. Net after lead spend: $10,000. CPA: $1,667.

Agent B wins on raw revenue per dollar — but Agent B needs the speed-to-lead infrastructure to close at 3% against captive shops with auto-dialers. Most independent IUL producers don't have it. Their effective real-time conversion is 1–1.5%, which collapses Agent B's math entirely.

At 1.5% real-time conversion: 100 records × 80% contact = 80 conversations × 1.5% = 1.2 sales × $5,000 = $6,000. Net: $1,000. CPA: $4,167. Aged wins by a wide margin.

For solo IUL producers or small financial planning operations, aged is the more defensible model. The consultative sales cycle is a feature, not a bug — it gives you the time to build the relationship and run the illustration, which is how IUL closes anyway.

Recommended cadence for aged IUL: 30–45 days, 12–15 touches. First touch: rate and concept intro. Follow-up sequence: retirement income angle, legacy planning, tax-advantaged growth. Email adds value here more than in FE — IUL buyers are more likely to engage written material before taking a call.

## How to allocate a life insurance lead budget

For a multi-product life agency running $5,000/month in lead spend:

- **50% final expense aged internet-form ($2,500).** At $1.25/lead, that's 2,000 records. Volume layer, gateway-product-then-cross-sell model.
- **25% aged IUL ($1,250).** At $5/lead, 250 records. Highest CPA payoff, longest sales cycle, smallest volume requirement.
- **15% aged term ($750).** At $8/lead, ~94 records. Gateway for younger buyers with cross-sell potential.
- **10% real-time FE live transfer ($500).** Premium close-day inventory for the front of the day, if you have the operational capacity to absorb the cost.

For a final-expense-only operation running $3,000/month:

- **60% direct mail response aged ($1,800).** At $15/lead average, ~120 records. Highest-quality tier for experienced FE telesales.
- **30% internet-form aged ($900).** At $1.25/lead, ~720 records. Volume layer.
- **10% live transfer real-time ($300).** Close-day premium inventory.

These aren't universal prescriptions. They're starting points. Track CPA by tier for 60–90 days and reweight toward the tier producing best margin in your specific market.

## Buying correctly: what to look for in a provider

For aged life insurance leads specifically, four things matter in a provider.

**Sub-vertical inventory depth.** You need a provider with real inventory in your specific tier — FE, term, and IUL are not interchangeable. Confirm filtering options for the sub-vertical you're buying, not just that they "sell life insurance leads."

**Transparent pricing.** Most aged lead vendors require a sales call to quote. Providers with published pricing let you run your own CPA math before you spend money. That transparency matters.

**Return policy with enforcement.** The aged lead category has a bad-data floor. An honest 10–20% return credit is standard. The question is whether the provider actually processes it consistently — not just what the policy says.

**Age bracket filtering.** The age bracket (31–85 days vs 86–180 days) changes the math substantially in life insurance. Make sure your provider lets you buy and filter by age, not just "aged leads" as a single category.

## What this means for your operation

Aged life insurance leads work. They work in all three sub-verticals, at different price points and different operational models. The agent who buys $1.25 FE records to run 60-dial days is making a sound economic decision. So is the solo IUL producer buying $5 records to run a consultative pipeline. They're just making different decisions.

A few things to take away before you start buying:

- Match the lead tier to your commission structure before you set a budget. Term agents running on $800–$1,200 FYC need tighter CPA discipline than IUL producers with a $5,000 ceiling.
- Life insurance leads age better than any other vertical. The 86–180 day tier is systematically under-bought. Test it before you write it off.
- Final expense is the volume play — cheap records, forgiving CPA, high call intensity. DM response aged is the quality play — expensive per record, high conversion, low call volume. Match the model to how you actually sell.
- Senior-demographic compliance (FE) requires tighter TCPA discipline than term or IUL. Build the consent documentation into your workflow from day one, not as an afterthought.
- IUL aged is the highest-leverage lead purchase in the category. If you sell IUL and you're still buying real-time, run the math above against your actual conversion rate. The outcome may surprise you.
