# Aged Life Insurance Leads: A Working Agent's Guide to the Three Sub-Markets

Life insurance agents hear a lot of the same advice about aged leads: buy them cheap, call them fast, and volume handles the rest. That advice is half right at best, and it collapses completely once you separate life insurance into its actual sub-markets.

There isn't one aged-life-insurance-lead market. There are three — final expense, term life, and indexed universal life — and each one has different pricing, different commission math, and a different answer to the only question that actually matters: what's the cost per acquisition my product's commission structure can sustain, and which lead tier delivers it?

Agents who treat these three sub-markets as one usually overpay on term, underinvest in IUL, and run final expense on a cadence built for mortgage leads. This article breaks them apart.

## Why Life Insurance Aged Leads Hold Value Longer Than Other Verticals

Before the sub-market breakdown, it's worth understanding why the aged lead math works differently for life insurance than for auto or mortgage.

Auto insurance policies renew every six months. If a prospect shopped in January and you're calling in August, there's a reasonable chance they already renewed somewhere else. Mortgage purchase leads carry a similar compression: the home decision resolves, one way or another, within a specific window.

Life insurance doesn't work that way. A 55-year-old woman who filled out a term life quote form in February to protect her mortgage is still 55 years old in August. Her mortgage balance hasn't changed. Her kids haven't grown up. The financial need that triggered the form fill is still there — it just went unanswered.

Final expense prospects are even stickier. A 70-year-old shopping for burial insurance in January is still concerned about that same conversation six months later. The trigger is permanent, not situational.

This is the structural insight that makes aged life insurance leads worth more at older ages than any comparable vertical. A 270-day-old life lead isn't stale the way a 270-day-old mortgage refi lead is stale. The clock doesn't run out on a need that was never going to go away.

## Final Expense Aged Leads — Where the Math Is Most Forgiving

Final expense is the most lead-accessible sub-market in life insurance, which is both its greatest advantage and its most common trap.

Monthly premiums run $30 to $80. First-year commissions (FYC) typically land between $500 and $1,500, with many carriers paying 110 to 120 percent of target premium in year one on small whole-life policies. The sales cycle is compressed — an experienced agent can close on a single call. Volume is the operating model.

**Pricing by tier:**

| Lead type | Age | Price range |
|---|---|---|
| Internet form | 31–85 days | $0.62–$1.88 each |
| Internet form | 86–365 days | $0.25–$0.40 each |
| Direct mail response | 31–85 days (exclusive) | $8–$25 each |

The direct mail response segment is the one most agents miss. These are consumers who took a physical action — they filled out and mailed back a reply card. The conversion rate reflects it. Where internet-form aged FE leads convert at roughly 1 to 2 percent of contacts to closed policies, direct mail response aged leads convert at 8 to 12 percent. Lower call volume, higher per-conversation yield, and a consumer who's demonstrated intent twice.

**The internet-form math:**

Agent A buys 500 aged FE internet leads at $1.25 each. Total spend: $625. Running a disciplined 10-touch cadence, she contacts 40 percent — 200 conversations. At 1.5 percent close-to-lead rate, that's 7 to 8 policies. Average FYC of $900. Gross: $6,300 to $7,200. Net after lead cost: $5,675 to $6,575.

**The direct mail aged math:**

Agent B buys 100 FE direct mail aged leads at $18 each. Total spend: $1,800. Contact rate is higher (these prospects are expecting outreach and haven't been called into shutdown). At 10 percent conversion: 10 policies. Average FYC of $900. Gross: $9,000. Net after lead cost: $7,200.

Two completely different operational modes. Both profitable. The direct mail route gets there faster with fewer dials; the internet-form route requires volume and cadence discipline to capture the spread.

One compliance note on FE specifically: the senior demographic carries heightened regulatory risk in several states. Florida's FTSA and Oklahoma's OTSA impose tighter restrictions on outreach to senior consumers. If you're running FE campaigns in FL, Mode A (conservative consent verification) is the right operating standard — original form-fill consent is the baseline, but FL requires you to review that consent documentation before each call. ## Term Life Aged Leads — The Volume Play With a Lower Ceiling

Term life is the mid-market product. Monthly premiums run $20 to $150 for healthy applicants. FYC lands between $400 and $2,000 depending on face amount and carrier. Unlike final expense, where a skilled agent can close in a single conversation, term life often takes two or three touches — the first call is discovery, the second is proposal, the third may be objection handling.

Aged internet-form term leads run $5 to $15 for the 31-to-85-day tier. The pricing reflects the higher competition on real-time term (multiple agents burn through the lead immediately) and the fact that many term shoppers are still in the research phase when they first submit a form.

**The term life math:**

Agent A buys 300 aged term leads at $8 each. Total spend: $2,400. 30 percent contact rate = 90 conversations. 2.5 percent close-to-lead = 7 to 8 policies. Average FYC of $900. Gross: $6,300 to $7,200. Net: $3,900 to $4,800.

The margin is tighter than FE because the FYC ceiling is lower. Term works best as the entry product in a multi-product agency — you're acquiring the client on term and converting to permanent coverage or cross-selling to other products over time. The cost-per-acquisition looks thin in isolation but makes sense when you account for policy duration and the back-book value of a term client who stays.

Where aged term falls apart: agents running a single-call FE cadence on term leads. Different consumer, different decision timeline, different call approach. The term prospect is usually more analytical — they want to compare, they have questions about carriers and underwriting, and they respond to email-first outreach better than cold calls.

## IUL Aged Leads — Where the Commission Ceiling Changes the Math Entirely

Indexed universal life is the high-net-worth sub-market, and aged IUL leads are the most underutilized asset in the aged lead category.

Monthly premiums start around $300 and run past $1,000 routinely. First-year commissions are $3,000 to $10,000 per policy — a single IUL closing funds six months of lead spend at FE volumes. The sales cycle is multi-touch, multi-week, and requires an educational posture rather than a closing posture.

Real-time IUL leads run $40 to $75 each. Aged IUL leads in the 31-to-85-day tier run $2 to $6. The discount is enormous.

**The aged IUL math:**

Agent A buys 500 aged IUL leads at $4 each. Total spend: $2,000. IUL prospects are harder to contact — assume 25 percent contact rate = 125 conversations. Conversion rate of 1 percent of total leads = 5 closed policies. Average FYC of $5,000. Gross: $25,000. Net after lead cost: $23,000.

Real-time IUL comparison at the same budget:

40 real-time IUL leads at $50 each = $2,000. Higher contact rate (40 percent) = 16 conversations. 10 percent close rate of conversations = 1.6 policies. At $5,000 FYC = $8,000. Net: $6,000.

The aged approach wins by a 3-to-1 margin on the same budget, because the commission ceiling is high enough that even a lower per-lead conversion rate produces more gross profit than the expensive real-time inventory. The exception: agents or teams with speed-to-lead infrastructure (power dialers, dedicated callers, instant follow-up systems). They can sustain real-time IUL economics. Most independent producers can't — which is why aged IUL is the smart play for solo operators.

The cadence for IUL is slower and more deliberate than FE or term. These prospects are evaluating a complex financial instrument. First call: discovery only. Understand whether they're interested in tax-advantaged accumulation, wealth transfer, or market-upside-with-floor protection. The second contact introduces a concept, not a proposal. The third frames the proposal. Zoom consultations outperform phone-only for IUL at any lead age — the product requires illustration, and illustration requires screen sharing.

## Cadence by Sub-Vertical

The biggest mistake agents make with aged life leads is running a single cadence across all three sub-markets. The timing, channel mix, and call approach need to match the product.

**Final expense (internet-form aged):** 10 touches in 30 days. Day 1, 2, 4, 7, 10, 14, 17, 21, 25, 30. Phone-first — FE is a conversational sale and the senior demographic responds to voice over email. Target contact windows: 9–11 AM and 4–6 PM in the prospect's time zone. For leads older than 180 days, run NCOA and skip-tracing before dialing to reduce disconnects.

**Term life (aged internet-form):** 8 touches in 21 days. Email on Day 1, then alternate phone and email. The term prospect is often employed and less reachable during business hours — evening and weekend morning attempts outperform lunch-hour calls. A short comparison-focused email ("here's what your rate looks like from three carriers — want to talk it through?") often converts more first responses than a cold call.

**IUL (aged internet-form):** 12 touches in 45 days. Email-heavy first 10 days (three value-add emails: one on how IUL works, one on a specific benefit concept, one on what you're going to show them). Then phone attempts starting Day 11. Text-to-email bridge for prospects who don't pick up. Offer a Zoom as the path-of-least-resistance next step.

For all three sub-verticals, the same compliance baseline applies: TCPA consent documentation needs to be reviewable before each call for the aged record, revocation requests honored immediately, and DNC scrubs on every batch. ## How to Allocate a Starting Budget

Operators new to aged life leads consistently make the same allocation mistake: they buy only one sub-vertical. Here's how a rational allocation looks by operating model.

**Multi-product life agency (term + FE + IUL):**
- 50% aged FE internet-form (cheap, high volume, good test environment for refining cadence)
- 25% aged IUL (highest net CPA return, lowest competition)
- 15% aged term (gateway product, cross-sell funnel entry)
- 10% aged FE direct mail response (premium FE inventory when you've refined your FE close)

**FE-only agency:**
- 60% aged FE direct mail response (highest quality, lowest call burden)
- 30% aged FE internet-form (volume, lower CPA per close even at lower conversion)
- 10% FE live transfer real-time (premium close-day inventory for high-capacity agents)

**Solo IUL specialist:**
- 100% aged IUL, minimum 300 leads to start. The volume threshold matters — IUL conversion is a single-digit percentage of contacts, and you need enough pipeline to run a 45-day educational sequence without burning out on non-answers.

Starting test budget for any of these: $500. That buys 400 aged FE internet-form leads at $1.25, or 125 aged IUL leads at $4, or 62 aged term leads at $8. Run all three for one month against your own cadence. Cost-per-contact and cost-per-appointment are the first signals. Cost-per-policy follows.

## What This Means for You

Three sub-markets, three different answers to the same question. Here's how to start:

- Pick the sub-vertical that matches your product — don't run FE cadence on IUL leads.
- Test aged internet-form first, direct mail aged second, live transfer last (expense increases with each tier; match it to your proven close rate).
- Run the CPA math before you buy. The formula: (lead cost × quantity) ÷ (expected conversion rate × average FYC). You're looking for 3–5x return on lead spend as your minimum acceptable threshold.
- For FE agents in Florida: review your consent documentation before every call. FL FTSA enforcement has increased. Mode A is the standard until you've consulted compliance counsel. [Henson Legal](https://www.henson-legal.com/) handles TCPA review for aged lead operators.
- Scale what works. Your first 30 days is a data collection exercise. The operator who scales on validated CPA math wins over the operator who guesses.
