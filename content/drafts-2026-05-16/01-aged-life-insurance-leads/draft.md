# Aged Life Insurance Leads in 2026: Final Expense, Term, and IUL — What the Economics Actually Look Like

Agents who buy aged life insurance leads often treat all three sub-markets as if they're the same product. They aren't. The economics of working an aged final expense lead are fundamentally different from the economics of working an aged term lead or an aged IUL lead — different conversion rate, different average premium, different optimal cadence, different contact-to-close timeline.

If you've bought a batch of aged life leads and the ROI looked worse than expected, there's a good chance the sub-type mismatch is why.

This is the guide for agents who want to understand what they're actually buying before they buy it, or who have already bought and want to know why the math isn't penciling out the way it should.

## The three life insurance sub-markets on aged leads

The lead industry sells "life insurance leads" as a single category. In practice it's three separate buying decisions.

**Final expense** leads originate from consumers age 50–85 shopping for burial and end-of-life coverage, typically $5,000–$25,000 face amounts, simple underwriting. These are high-volume, low-ticket, fast-cycle leads. Final expense is the dominant sub-type in the aged life market — roughly 60–70% of what you'll find on major aged lead marketplaces.

**Term life** leads come from consumers requesting quotes on 10-, 20-, or 30-year policies, usually younger buyers (25–55), and often driven by a life event: new mortgage, marriage, new child. Average policy premium is higher than final expense, but so is the competition on fresh leads — which is one reason term leads age well. A 90-day-old term inquiry is still a real buyer who still has that mortgage.

**IUL (Indexed Universal Life)** leads represent a smaller volume but higher-ticket subset — consumers who were pitched on the savings/accumulation angle of permanent life. These are the longest sales cycles in the life insurance category, but they also carry the highest commission per close. Aged IUL leads require a different approach than the other two.

Most aged-lead marketplaces blend all three. Before you buy, ask what the mix looks like, or filter aggressively by form type.

## What aged life insurance leads actually are

An aged life insurance lead is a consumer record — name, phone, email, state, product type, coverage amount requested, health tier — that was originally submitted on an internet comparison form and sold to its first round of buyers within minutes. By the time you're accessing it, the record is anywhere from 30 to 365 days old. The original buyers have moved on.

What hasn't changed: the consumer's underlying situation. The person who needed burial coverage in January likely still needs burial coverage in April. The 38-year-old who wanted a 20-year term to protect his family's new house hasn't stopped needing that policy because six agents called him in the first week.

This is the central insight that makes aged life leads work: the intent was real. The problem is timing and follow-up saturation, not intent. You're calling after the initial feeding frenzy. The competition-adjusted conversion rate for a well-worked aged life lead is often comparable to or better than a real-time shared lead at four to eight times the price.

## Pricing by sub-type: what you'll pay in 2026

Aged life insurance lead prices vary based on three factors: sub-type, age bracket, and filter stack.

**Final expense aged leads**: $0.50–$3.50 per record. Under 90 days tends to run $2.00–$3.50. 90–365 days drops to $0.50–$1.50. Volume pricing ($500+ orders) unlocks better rates.

**Term life aged leads**: $1.00–$5.00 per record. Higher pricing than final expense reflects the larger policy size and higher original real-time lead value. Under 60 days and filtered by state and coverage amount can push toward the top of that range.

**IUL aged leads**: $2.00–$8.00 per record. Smaller supply, higher ticket, narrower audience. These tend to be more regionally concentrated and harder to buy in bulk.

These are ranges — the actual price you pay depends on marketplace, minimum order, and whether you're filtering on geography or other criteria. The benchmark that matters more than the per-lead cost is what you'll find in the next section.

## The unit economics — CPA math for each sub-type

Per-lead cost is the wrong number to optimize. The number that matters is cost per acquisition — cost per closed policy. Let me show you what that actually looks like.

**Final expense example.** Assume you buy 500 leads at $1.00 per record. Total cost: $500. Average conversion rate for well-worked aged final expense: 2–5%. At 3%, that's 15 closed policies out of 500 leads. Average final expense commission on a $10,000 policy with a standard carrier contract: $400–$600. Call it $500.

Cost per closing: $500 ÷ 15 = **$33.33 per closed policy**.
Commission per policy: $500.
Net per policy (after lead cost): $466.67.
Total net on the batch (at 3% conversion): **$7,000 on a $500 investment**.

That math looks improbable until you track a real campaign. It's not. Final expense is the highest-volume aged-lead play in the life category precisely because the per-lead economics are this compressed.

**Term life example.** 200 leads at $3.00 per record. Total cost: $600. Aged term conversion rates are typically lower than final expense — 1–3% on a well-worked list. At 2%, that's 4 closed policies. Average commission on a 20-year term with a $500,000 face amount: $900–$1,400 first-year. Call it $1,100.

Cost per closing: $600 ÷ 4 = **$150 per closed policy**.
Commission per policy: $1,100.
Net per policy: $950.
Total net on the batch: **$3,800 on a $600 investment**.

The per-lead cost is higher, the conversion rate is lower, but the per-policy revenue is significantly higher. The risk profile is different — you're counting on fewer, larger closes.

**IUL example.** 100 leads at $5.00. Total cost: $500. Conversion on aged IUL is the lowest of the three — 0.5–1.5% is realistic. At 1%, that's 1 closed policy. Average IUL commission on a policy with a $300/month premium: $2,000–$4,000 first-year. Call it $2,500.

Cost per closing: $500 ÷ 1 = **$500 per closed policy**.
Commission per policy: $2,500.
Net per policy: $2,000.

The CPA is higher in raw dollars, but so is the return per close. IUL aged leads are the highest-risk, highest-reward play — not the right starting point for an agent new to the aged-lead model.

## Final expense: the clearest entry point

Final expense is where most agents start with aged life leads, and with good reason. The economics are the most predictable, the product is straightforward (simplified issue, no medical exam, quick approval), and the buyer profile matches what aged leads provide: someone older, still needing coverage, who got frustrated with the initial agent contact and went cold.

A few things that make aged final expense work better than newer agents expect:

Volume is the lever. At $0.75–$1.50 per lead, you can buy 300–500 records for $300–$500. That's enough to run a real test of your cadence. With final expense, the math only becomes visible at scale — a 10-lead sample tells you almost nothing.

The contact rate is your primary KPI in week one. If you're not reaching 30–40% of your list on calls and texts within the first 14 days, the problem is the cadence, not the leads. Aged final expense buyers are reachable — they submitted a real form, they gave a real phone number. A 10-20% contact rate usually means the dialing volume is too low or the call windows are wrong (call before 9am or after 5pm local, and you'll see it improve).

The opening frame matters more than the script. "I'm calling about your request for burial insurance coverage" reframes the call from a cold pitch to a follow-up. That single word — *follow-up* — changes the conversation's starting position.

## Term life: the misunderstood middle

Term life aged leads get less attention than final expense, which is partly why they're an underworked opportunity. The objection most agents raise is that term buyers are more price-sensitive and harder to close without a current rate quote. That's true. It's also manageable.

The case for aged term: the life event that drove the original inquiry — mortgage, marriage, new child — often hasn't resolved. A 90-day-old term inquiry from someone who just bought a house is still a 90-day-old mortgage with nobody on the other end of the life insurance decision. The intent hasn't expired. What expired is the buyer's patience with agents who called once and disappeared.

Aged term leads require a slightly longer cadence and a different framing. You're not re-selling them on *why they need term insurance*. They know why. You're re-opening the conversation on the decision they never finished making. The opener that works: "You requested a term quote back in [month]. I want to make sure you got something squared away."

One distinction worth making: term leads aged 8–60 days work differently from leads aged 120–365 days. The 30-day term lead is still in an active buying window in most cases; the 9-month-old term lead is almost certainly either already covered or has mentally deferred the decision. Filter for age appropriately.

## IUL: the high-ticket play for experienced operators

Aged IUL leads are not where you start. They require a longer nurture cycle — 60–90 days from first contact to close is typical, vs. 2–3 weeks for final expense — and the sales conversation is more complex. You're discussing accumulation, market participation caps, loan provisions, and retirement income projections. An agent who hasn't worked IUL fresh is going to struggle with the conversational complexity on aged leads.

That said, if you have the skills and the patience: the commission math is compelling. A $300/month premium IUL policy with a competent carrier relationship is worth $2,000–$4,000 in first-year comp. At $3–8 per aged lead, your cost per acquisition is manageable even at a 1% conversion rate if you're willing to work a 90-day pipeline.

The one structural thing to get right on IUL: the follow-up has to include educational content, not just call attempts. An aged IUL prospect who doesn't hear from you for three weeks will not self-serve their way to a decision. Email is a meaningful part of the cadence for this sub-type.

## Cadence: what 14 days of follow-up looks like for life insurance

The cadence is where the difference between a 1% conversion rate and a 4% conversion rate lives. Here's the skeleton that works across all three sub-types; the timing is the same, the messaging adjusts.

**Day 1:** Call (local presence dialer if possible) + text immediately after voicemail. Frame as a follow-up to their original inquiry.
**Day 2:** Email — brief, plain text, 2 sentences, no attachments. The goal is one reply.
**Day 3:** Call + voicemail.
**Day 5:** Text only. "Still available to help if you're still looking."
**Day 7:** Call. If you reach them: this is your real first conversation.
**Day 10:** Email #2. Slightly more specific to their product type (FE, term, or IUL).
**Day 14:** Final call attempt + text. Archive if no response.

Six touches over 14 days. For IUL leads, extend the cadence to 30 days with 2–3 additional email touches after day 14.

## What to look for when buying aged life leads

Not all aged life lead inventories are the same. The variables that determine lead quality:

**Original form source.** Internet-form leads (consumer submitted an online quote request) are the strongest aged-life product. Avoid "compiled" lists, co-registration data, or anything that doesn't trace back to a product-specific form submission. Ask the vendor.

**Age bracket.** 30–90 days is the sweet spot for most agents — recent enough to have current phone/email, old enough to have left the real-time buying frenzy. The 8–30 day range is competitive and pricier; the 180–365 day range requires higher volume to get the same closings.

**Geography filters.** Filter to states where you're licensed. This sounds obvious, but many buyers skip geography filters to drive per-lead cost down, then spend call time on leads they can't legally write.

**Exclusive vs. shared.** Most aged internet leads are shared — they were sold to multiple buyers originally and may have been in other agents' CRMs. True exclusive aged leads at the $3–8 range exist but are a smaller portion of inventory. For most operators, shared aged at $0.75–$2.00 per record outperforms exclusive aged at $4–8 purely on volume economics.

**DNC scrub.** Confirm the vendor scrubs against the National Do-Not-Call Registry. This is basic TCPA hygiene. Any vendor not doing this automatically is not worth buying from. ## Monday morning action list

If you're starting fresh with aged life insurance leads:

- Decide your sub-type. Final expense is the clearest entry point for new operators. Term if you have existing carrier contracts and a comfort with underwriting conversations. IUL only if you have the skills and cycle-time tolerance.
- Budget for volume, not quality. $300 in aged final expense leads (200–300 records) is a better first test than $300 in aged IUL (30–50 records). The math requires scale.
- Build the 14-day cadence before you buy. Have your voicemail script, your text template, and your two email templates ready on day one. The leads decay in value if they sit in your CRM while you write scripts.
- Track cost per closing, not conversion rate. Conversion rate is a useful secondary metric. Cost per closing is what tells you whether the economics actually work for your business.
- Sort your list before you dial. If you have AI lead scoring available, use it — higher-probability records first. If you don't, sort by geography (work your home state first) and by age of lead (30–90 days before 90–365 days). For a full scoring methodology, see [AI Lead Scoring for Aged Lists](/blog/ai-lead-scoring-aged-leads).

Aged life insurance leads work. The agents who report that they don't are usually running a cadence that's too short, buying too few leads to see the math, or mixing sub-types without tracking them separately. Fix any one of those three things and the economics change.
