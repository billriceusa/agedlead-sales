# Aged Life Insurance Leads: The Operator's Buying and Working Guide (2026)

Every life insurance agent eventually hits the same wall. Real-time leads are expensive and come loaded with competition — by the time the lead hits your dialer, three to five other agents already know this person's name. Fresh exclusive leads are better, but at $30-$75 per record in the term life space and $20-$50 for final expense, the math is tight the moment your close rate dips.

Aged life insurance leads are the channel most agents either ignore entirely or buy once, dial wrong, and write off as a failure. The ones who learn to work them — correctly, at scale — run some of the most profitable life books in the country on budgets that would buy them maybe fifteen real-time leads a week.

This guide covers everything: what aged life leads are, what they cost, how the ROI compares when you run real numbers, how to filter a buy, and what a working cadence actually looks like.

## What aged life insurance leads are

An aged life insurance lead is a consumer who submitted an internet form requesting information about life coverage — term life, final expense, IUL, mortgage protection — anywhere from 8 days to 12 months ago. That form was sold to its original real-time buyers within minutes or hours of submission. Now it's available through the aged marketplace at a fraction of its original price.

The data is the same record. Name, phone numbers, email, coverage amount requested, age, sometimes health class tier — whatever the original form captured, you get. What's different is what happened between then and now: the real-time buyers chased the lead for a few days, the pipeline moved on, and the prospect largely stopped getting calls. Studies of large aged-lead databases consistently show that 70% of aged life and health leads were contacted by only one agent in the real-time window. Less than 5% received more than three meaningful attempts.

That's not a burned lead. That's an undercalled one.

## What they cost — and what drives the price

Aged life insurance leads sell across a wide price band, depending on three variables: vertical, age of the record, and the vendor's data hygiene.

Here's what the current market looks like:

| Lead type | Age | Price range (US market) |
|---|---|---|
| Real-time internet form (term life) | Same-day | $20–$65 |
| Real-time internet form (final expense) | Same-day | $15–$50 |
| Real-time exclusive (any life vertical) | Same-day | $40–$120 |
| Aged internet form (any life vertical) | 8–30 days | $3–$12 |
| Aged internet form | 31–90 days | $1–$6 |
| Aged internet form | 91–180 days | $0.50–$3 |
| Aged internet form | 181–365 days | $0.25–$2 |

The sticker-price gap between aged and real-time runs 10x to 50x at the median. A 60-day-old final expense lead at $1.50 and a same-day version of the same form at $30 are the same person, the same intent, the same data. The extra $28.50 on the real-time version is paying for freshness and for the privilege of competing with four other agents who got the same lead at the same time.

Sticker price isn't the number that matters. Cost per closing is.

## The math: two agents, same $1,500 budget

Agent A buys real-time final expense leads. Her cost is $30 per lead, shared with three other agents. Budget: $1,500. Leads: 50. With a typical 3% close rate on shared real-time FEX leads, she expects 1.5 closes from that buy — call it 1 deal reliably. Issued premium on a $60/month FEX policy: $720/year. Cost per closing: $1,500.

Agent B buys aged final expense leads at $1.50 per record, 90-day-old data, from a vendor who scrubs for disconnects and appends additional phone numbers. Budget: $1,500. Leads: 1,000. Aged FEX leads in the 3-month window convert at roughly 1.5% to 2% with a systematic 7-touch cadence. At 1.5%: 15 closes. Same $60/month issued premium per deal: 15 × $720 = $10,800 in year-one issued premium against the same $1,500 spend.

The math is not close. It never is when you run it honestly.

Agent A's real-time conversion rate looks better on paper — 3% vs. 1.5%. But "competition-adjusted conversion rate" — what you actually close after accounting for the other agents working the same list — is the number that determines profitability, and on shared real-time leads, that number craters. Agent B has no competition because nobody else is working her buy.

Volume changes everything when your cost is low enough. This is the central truth of aged lead economics.

## How to evaluate and buy aged life leads

Not all aged life lead vendors are the same. The differences that matter most:

**Data hygiene and phone append.** Aged records accumulate disconnected numbers. A vendor who doesn't scrub and append additional phone numbers — landlines, cell, relatives in household — will deliver a high percentage of dead dials. Look for vendors who explicitly describe their phone validation process and provide at least two to three numbers per record.

**Vertical specificity.** Aged internet-form databases contain life leads, FEX leads, health leads, and IUL leads. Make sure you're filtering by the right form type. "Life leads" is not "final expense leads" — the buyer profiles, average ages, and conversations are different. Buy vertical-specific inventory when you can.

**Lead age range.** There's no single optimal age. For agents who work a high-volume dialing model, 30–90 day records often deliver the best balance of price and contactability. For agents who prefer a more targeted approach with email + phone, 91–180 day records are cheaper and still highly workable. The 180–365 day tier has the widest margins but requires the most patience — the prospects are real, but you're deeper into re-establishing context.

**Geography and filtering.** Reputable vendors let you filter by state, county, zip code radius, age of prospect, and sometimes by original coverage amount or health class. Buy into your licensed states only. If you're working final expense, filter for ages 55–80; if term life, 25–55 is the core age band.

**No minimum orders and transparent pricing.** The best vendors in the aged life space publish their per-lead pricing without requiring a sales call. Self-service access to inventory, no contracts, and the ability to start with a small test buy (100–200 records) is the baseline. Any vendor who won't show you pricing before asking for your credit card deserves skepticism.

## How to work aged life leads: the cadence

This is where most operators fail. They buy a list, dial it once, get a 3% contact rate on Day 1, and conclude that aged leads don't work. A single dial attempt on aged data is not a strategy — it's a test of your phone's battery.

A viable cadence for aged life insurance leads looks like this:

**Day 1**: Call attempt 1 (morning, before 10 AM local). If no answer, leave no voicemail. Note the result.

**Day 2**: Call attempt 2 (late afternoon, 4–6 PM local). If no answer, text: *"Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] with [Agency]. You requested info on life insurance coverage a while back — wanted to see if you're still looking. Reply STOP to opt out."*

**Day 4**: Call attempt 3. If no answer, leave a short voicemail referencing the original inquiry: *"Hi [Name], this is [Your Name]. You asked about life coverage — I have a few options I'd love to walk you through. Give me a call back at [number]."*

**Day 7**: Email outreach. Plain text, no images, no heavy HTML. Subject line: *"Life insurance — still interested?"*. Keep it two sentences.

**Day 10–14**: Final call attempt. If still no contact, flag for a 30-day re-queue.

The 7-touch approach — mixing calls, texts, and emails across multiple days — typically produces 3x to 4x the contact rate of a single-day dial-through. That lift in contact rate is directly proportional to your conversion rate, because you can't close someone you never reach.

## Compliance: what you need to know for 2026

The FCC's 1:1 consent rule — which would have required a separate, per-seller consent on every internet form lead — was vacated in January 2025 and formally repealed in August 2025. The practical implication: aged internet-form leads with a valid one-to-many consent clause are legally workable under current federal TCPA framework.

What does matter in 2026:

**State mini-TCPAs.** Florida (FTSA), Oklahoma (OTSA), Washington (HB 1497), Maryland, and Texas each have state-level provisions that go beyond federal TCPA. If you're dialing into these states, you need to review the specific consent requirements. [Henson Legal](https://www.henson-legal.com/) is the TCPA counsel we reference across this site for agent compliance questions.

**Revocation.** The FCC's revocation-of-consent rule — requiring prompt opt-out processing — was delayed to January 2027, but the consumer expectation is already there. Have a clean opt-out process for texts and emails. Honor it immediately when triggered.

**DNC scrubbing.** Aged leads must be scrubbed against the federal DNC registry before dialout. Any reputable vendor will confirm their list has been run against DNC; you should also run your own scrub if you're using a dialer.

The practical framing: aged internet-form life leads sit in what I call **Mode B** — operator standard. That means valid form consent, DNC-scrubbed, with a respectful multi-touch cadence and no auto-dialing without independent consent verification. It's not the most conservative possible approach, but it's where the large majority of professional life insurance agents operate safely and profitably. If you want Mode A — the most conservative posture — that means outbound cold calling only, no texting on aged data without fresh revocation-scrubbed consent. Mode A is slower; the economics are thinner.

Run it how you run your business. Just run it with accurate information.

## What this means for your next move

Aged life insurance leads aren't a shortcut. They're a volume play that requires process: clean data, a multi-touch cadence, and enough leads in the funnel to let the math work. Three hundred records and a single dial attempt is not a test. Three hundred records, a seven-touch sequence, and three weeks of follow-up — that's a test.

Here's where to start:

- **Set a test budget of $300–$500.** At $1–$3 per record (30–90 day vintage), you're buying 100–300 aged life leads. That's enough to establish a real contact rate and get 2–4 closes if your cadence is right.
- **Filter tight.** Pick one state, one product (term or FEX, not both), one age band. Keep the test clean so you can read the results.
- **Run at least 6 touches per record before evaluating.** If you only dial once, you're measuring voicemail quality, not lead quality.
- **Track cost per closing, not contact rate.** The number that tells you whether to scale is the cost per issued policy, not the percentage of dials answered.
