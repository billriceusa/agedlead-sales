# Aged Final Expense Leads: What They Cost, How to Work Them, and the Math That Makes Them Worth It

Most final expense agents are spending $8–25 per direct mail response lead. They've been doing it for years. The process works — response cards come back, you call the pile — and they've calibrated their whole operation around it.

What they haven't done is run the math against aged internet-form leads. When they do, the comparison is uncomfortable.

A 30–90-day aged internet FE lead costs $0.62–1.88. The person on the other end filled out a form requesting final expense information — same intent signal as a direct mail card, different origination channel. The math doesn't change the intent. It changes the acquisition cost by an order of magnitude.

This isn't an argument that aged internet leads are better than direct mail in every dimension. They aren't. But if you're spending $5,000 a month on direct mail and you've never pressure-tested the ROI against aged internet leads, you're making a budget decision without all the information.

## What Aged Final Expense Leads Actually Are

An aged final expense lead is a record of a consumer who requested information about final expense or burial insurance — typically through an online quote form, an insurance aggregator, or a call-to-action ad — and whose record was sold to one or more real-time buyers at or near submission, then entered the aged marketplace some time later.

The record carries the original intent signal: the consumer's name, phone, email, age, state, and the product they were researching. What changes with age is the price and the competitive window. The original real-time buyers called within minutes of submission. Most gave up after two or three attempts. By the time you're calling at day 45 or day 90, the herd is gone.

That distinction matters for how you work them. Aged internet FE leads are **not** the same as:

- **Direct mail response leads** — a different origination channel with different demographics (DM skews older, often rural, lower-tech); DM response leads are their own product category
- **Telemarketing-generated leads** — outbound-originated, different consent basis
- **Live transfers** — phone-verified, warm hand-offs, priced at $50–80 for a reason

Aged internet leads sit between direct mail and live transfers in intent quality. They're colder than a warm transfer, warmer than a cold scrub list. The job is to efficiently reach enough of them that the math works in your favor.

## 2026 Pricing: Where the Market Sits

The final expense lead market in 2026 looks like this:

| Lead type | Age / channel | Typical range |
|---|---|---|
| Internet form | Real-time, shared | $15–65 |
| Live transfer | Real-time, exclusive | $50–80 |
| Direct mail response | Exclusive, fresh | $8–25 |
| Internet form | 8–30 days, shared | $1.50–5 |
| Internet form | 31–90 days, shared | $0.62–1.88 |
| Internet form | 91–180 days, shared | $0.25–1.25 |

A few things to note about that table. First, the "real-time" internet row has a wide spread — $15–65 — because the FE real-time market includes everything from raw aggregator leads to semi-exclusive leads scrubbed for contactability. The cheaper end of that range is competitive and shared; the expensive end is tighter supply. Second, direct mail leads priced at $8–25 are exclusive by default (the mailer came back to your address), which is a real advantage — but you're paying for that exclusivity. Third, aged internet leads at $0.62–1.88 are shared, which means other agents may have already worked the same record. In the final expense market that's less of a disadvantage than it sounds, because most agents under-contact — three calls and a voicemail is not a cadence.

## The Math: Two Agents, Same $500 Budget

This is where most agents stop reading their direct mail invoices and start paying attention.

**Agent A — buys direct mail response leads**

Budget: $500. Lead cost: $12.50 each (midpoint). Leads purchased: 40. Expected contact rate on first-pass dialing (DM leads): 35%. Contacts: 14. Conversion rate (FE close rate from contact, experienced agent): 20%. Sales: 2.8 — call it 3 on a good month, 2 on an average one.

At $750 average FYC per sale (midpoint FE commission), gross revenue = $1,500–2,250. Net of lead spend: $1,000–1,750. Cost per acquisition (CPA): $167–250.

**Agent B — buys aged internet leads, 31–90 day**

Budget: $500. Lead cost: $1.25 each (midpoint). Leads purchased: 400. Expected contact rate over a six-touch, 10-day cadence: 22%. Contacts: 88. Conversion rate from contact (slightly lower than DM — aged internet contacts are cooler): 14%. Sales: 12.3 — average month.

At $750 FYC per sale, gross revenue = $9,225. Net of lead spend: $8,725. CPA: $41.

Read those numbers again. Same $500. Same FYC. Agent A gets 2–3 sales. Agent B closes 12. The CPA gap — $200 vs $41 — is the mathematical argument for aged internet leads.

The catch is obvious: Agent B needs to actually run a six-touch cadence across 400 records in 10 days. That requires a dialer, a CRM, and a process. It's not a passive operation. But neither is direct mail — you're just trading phone work for mail logistics and a 4-week cycle.

## Buying Right: What Filters Actually Matter

Not all aged FE lead sources are equivalent. When you're evaluating a vendor or building a filter set, these are the fields that move the math:

**Age of lead.** 31–90 days is the sweet spot for most agents. Fresh enough that the consumer's circumstances haven't changed dramatically; old enough that the original buyer competition has cleared. Beyond 180 days, contact rates drop materially — not impossible to work, just a different math set.

**Lead source.** Internet form leads from insurance aggregators, comparison sites, and direct-response ads tend to have the cleanest intent signal — the consumer clicked, filled out a form, and provided real information. Be skeptical of "internet leads" that were actually scraped, list-appended, or co-reg sourced. Ask the vendor how the original lead was generated.

**Data completeness.** At minimum: first name, phone, email, ZIP, age, and stated product interest. Leads missing phone or with only a cell number (and no DNC scrub) require extra TCPA diligence. Aged leads with a verified active phone on file are worth more per record — factor that into pricing comparisons.

**Geography.** If you're licensed in 5 states, filter to those states. This sounds obvious, but vendors will sell you national batches if you let them.

**TCPA status.** Ask whether leads have been scrubbed against the National DNC Registry and, if relevant, whether original consent language covered text and calls separately. More on the compliance framing below.

## The Cadence: Six Touches in Ten Days

This is the part that separates agents who "tried aged leads and they didn't work" from agents who run a real operation.

Aged internet FE leads don't convert on the first call. Neither do direct mail leads, in practice — most agents just don't count the number of attempts they make before giving up. The difference is that with aged internet leads, you have enough volume that touching each record six times is the difference between $41 CPA and no result at all.

Here's the cadence structure that works in final expense:

**Day 1 — Call 1 (morning, 9–11am local time):** Live call. FE prospects skew 50–85 years old — mornings are significantly better than evenings, and weekdays are better than weekends. Leave a brief voicemail if no answer: name, callback number, reference to the insurance inquiry.

**Day 1 — Text (same afternoon):** Short text after the voicemail. "Hi [Name], I tried calling about your final expense insurance inquiry. Would [tomorrow] morning work for a quick call? — [Your name]." State-law note: confirm text is permitted under your consent documentation before sending.

**Day 2 — Email (morning):** Drip email acknowledging the inquiry, brief explanation of final expense coverage options, soft call to action. No hard sell. The email builds familiarity before Call 2.

**Day 3 — Call 2 (late morning):** Second live call attempt. Different time window than Day 1.

**Day 5 — Call 3 + email follow-up.**

**Day 10 — Final call + re-qualification attempt:** If no contact after five attempts, this is the clean-up call. Different framing: "I've been trying to reach you about your insurance inquiry and want to make sure I'm not missing something — happy to help or close out the file."

Six touches. Ten days. Most agents stop at two calls and declare the lead dead. The data suggests the third, fourth, and fifth contact is where a disproportionate number of FE conversions happen — the prospect was traveling, was in the hospital, or simply wasn't ready on the first pass.

## The Senior Contact Window

Final expense is almost exclusively sold to consumers between 50 and 85. That demographic has distinct contact patterns that most generic aged-lead advice ignores.

Morning calls (9–11am local) outperform evening calls by a significant margin in this age group. Seniors who filled out an internet form are more phone-literate than stereotype suggests — they searched, they clicked, they filled out a form — but they also have more routine-driven days. Early afternoon (1–2pm) is a secondary window. After 4pm, contact rates drop. Weekends are generally weaker for FE aged internet leads than for direct mail, which skews more rural and routine-disrupted.

The voicemail strategy matters more here than in any other vertical. A 50-year-old who gets a voicemail from an unknown number will often screen and not call back. A specific, clear, non-threatening voicemail — "this is about the final expense information you requested" — performs materially better than a generic callback request.

## A Note on Compliance

Final expense agents face real TCPA exposure. They're calling older consumers who may be on the DNC, they're often using dialers that approach autodialer classification, and the FE market has had its share of enforcement actions.

The cleanest operating posture — Mode A in our framework — is prior express written consent obtained separately for each carrier or agent at the point of the original form submission. That documentation requirement is non-trivial, but it's the only path that's genuinely bulletproof at both the federal level and under state mini-TCPAs in FL, OK, WA, MD, and TX.

The practical implication for aged lead buyers: ask your vendor whether the original consent language at the time of form submission was specific enough to cover your outreach. Many forms used broad "I agree to be contacted" language that was standard practice before the FCC's rules tightened. Leads generated with that language are not automatically non-compliant — the FCC's 1:1 consent rule was vacated in January 2025 and repealed in August 2025 — but individual state laws may impose higher standards. If you're in Florida or Oklahoma, the state-specific FTSA/OTSA exposure deserves a separate conversation with counsel.

## What to Do on Monday

If you've been running on direct mail and want to pressure-test aged internet leads without blowing up your pipeline:

- Start with a $200–400 test batch of 31–90-day aged FE internet leads, filtered to your licensed states and your preferred age range (50–79 is a common entry band).
- Set a minimum data standard: phone + email + ZIP + stated age on every record. No exceptions.
- Run the six-touch, 10-day cadence on every lead. Don't declare anything dead at two calls.
- Track contact rate, quotes generated, and closes separately. After 60 days, run your actual CPA against your direct mail CPA. The comparison will tell you what to scale.
- Don't abandon direct mail if it's working — the goal is to know your cost per closing in each channel, then allocate budget accordingly.
