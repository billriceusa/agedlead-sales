# How to Work Aged Life Insurance Leads: A 30-Day Cadence That Actually Closes

The agent who buys 500 final expense leads and calls each record once — then writes off the channel because "the leads are garbage" — has made a system error. Not a lead-quality diagnosis.

Aged life insurance leads require a different operating model than real-time leads. That's not a defect. It's the reason they cost $1.50 to $5 per record instead of $30 to $75. The economics are so favorable precisely because most agents run the wrong cadence, confirm their assumption that aged leads don't work, and go back to buying expensive inventory at thin margins. The agents running the right system clean up on the inventory those agents abandoned.

This is the system.

## Why aged life insurance leads need a different approach

Real-time leads are speed-to-lead plays. You have a narrow window — often under five minutes before two or three other agents reach the same prospect — and the competitive pressure is baked into the economics. Pay $40 for a term life lead, call within three minutes, convert or lose it.

Aged leads work the opposite way. The prospect submitted a form 30 to 90 days ago. They've forgotten it. They may have been called by a few agents in the first 72 hours and then went quiet — which is why you have the record at all. The approach that wins isn't speed. It's persistence and warm professional contact over a structured sequence of touches.

The other key difference: aged life insurance leads hold their conversion potential longer than aged leads in almost any other vertical. A 90-day-old auto insurance prospect may have already renewed their existing policy. A 90-day-old mortgage purchase prospect may have already bought a home. A 90-day-old final expense prospect is still the same age with the same financial situation. The underlying need hasn't resolved — it's just been sitting.

That changes how you think about the 30-day cadence. You're not racing against expiration. You're finding the right moment to have a conversation the prospect is now actually ready to have.

## The setup that determines whether your cadence works

Before the first dial, three things have to be right.

**DNC scrub.** Mandatory before any outreach begins. Pull your list through the National Do Not Call Registry. In final expense specifically — where your prospect pool skews 60 to 80 years old — you're also operating in states with stricter rules than federal law. Florida's FTSA, Washington's HB 1497, and Texas mini-TCPA rules impose additional restrictions on calls to certain senior demographics. Don't skip state-level scrubbing if you're working those markets.

**Bad-data purge.** Aged leads carry a bad-data rate of roughly 10-20%, depending on lead age bracket and source. Don't dilute your cadence with records you can't work. On day one, run your list through a basic phone validation service. Flag disconnects, invalid formats, and known landline-to-cell mismatches. Set those aside for return credit if your provider has a return policy. What's left is your dialable batch.

**CRM tagging.** Before you touch the phone, tag every record in your CRM with the lead age bracket (31-60 day, 61-85 day, 86+ day) and the sub-vertical product (term, final expense, IUL). The cadence is different for each combination — which you'll see in the next sections. If your CRM can't support that tagging, build a spreadsheet that can. The segmentation is not optional; it's what allows you to measure whether your cadence is working by sub-vertical rather than averaging across the whole batch.

**Volume math.** One agent can work approximately 80 to 100 records per day through a structured multi-touch cadence without it becoming either robotic or unsustainable. If you have 1,000 records and one agent, that's a 10- to 12-day working window on the initial touch sequence, not a single day. Buy accordingly — don't buy inventory you can't run a proper sequence on.

## The final expense cadence: 30 days, 8 to 10 touches

Final expense is the highest-volume aged-lead sub-vertical for a reason. The prospect pool (50 to 80, fixed income, health-conscious) is at home during business hours, responsive to multi-touch follow-up, and not in active rate-shop mode at the 30-day mark the way term-life buyers often are. The cadence reflects that.

**Days 1 to 3 — three call attempts, different time windows.**
Morning (8:30–10:30 AM), mid-day (12:30–2:00 PM), early evening (5:00–7:00 PM). Spread your three initial attempts across all three windows before concluding someone isn't answering. Many agents stop after one attempt at their default call time and miss the prospects who are only reachable at odd hours.

No voicemail on attempts one and two. On attempt three, leave a brief voicemail that references the original inquiry without being heavy-handed: "Hi, this is [name] with [agency]. You had requested some information on life insurance coverage — I just wanted to make sure you got what you needed. Feel free to call me back at [number], or I'll try you again in a few days."

**Days 4 to 7 — email and text, light touch.**
One email and one text per record over this window. The email should introduce yourself, reference the inquiry, and offer a one-question opener: "Many people I talk to in your area are primarily concerned with making sure their family doesn't carry the cost of final arrangements. Is that what you were looking into?" The goal isn't to close — it's to get a reply that tells you whether there's a real conversation to have.

Text messages in this window should be short and non-promotional: "Hi [name], I left you a voicemail earlier this week about your life insurance inquiry. Is there a better time to connect? — [name], [agency]." In states with stricter text restrictions (FL, WA), confirm opt-in status before sending.

**Days 8 to 14 — two more call attempts, voicemail optional.**
If you've had no response, two more calls in this window. If you reach voicemail on either, leave a second message — slightly more specific this time about what you can offer. If you've had any response (email reply, text back, answered call that didn't convert), priority-tag this record and work it actively in this window before moving it to a longer-term drip.

**Days 15 to 21 — three-email drip sequence.**
For records that haven't engaged, deploy a three-email drip over this period. Each email covers a different angle: Email 1 — what final expense insurance actually covers (education-first, no hard sell). Email 2 — a worked scenario showing the difference between a family with coverage and one without at time of death. Email 3 — a simple call-to-action: "I have five minutes. Can we find one?"

**Days 22 to 30 — final attempt and folder decision.**
One final call attempt. If you reach someone who is definitively uninterested, close the record with a courtesy note and set a 90-day revisit reminder — life circumstances change. If the record has never engaged in 30 days and all data checks out as valid, fold it and move on. At scale, the 10 to 15 percent who do engage in the first 30 days are your conversion pipeline; the rest are not failures, they're volume math.

The expected output across a 1,000-record FE batch at this cadence: roughly 100 to 150 contacts over 30 days, 25 to 40 appointments set, 12 to 20 closed policies depending on experience and vertical alignment. At a $550 average final expense FYC and $1,500 in lead spend (1,000 leads at $1.50 each), the math works out to a CPA of $75 to $125 per closed policy — against a product where the break-even CPA is typically north of $200.

## The term life cadence: compress to 14 days

Term life buyers make decisions faster than final expense buyers. They're often younger (30 to 55), digitally active, and in an active shopping mode that resolves one way or another within a few weeks of the original inquiry. A 60-day-old term life lead has usually either bought from someone else, changed their mind, or moved to a different product.

That changes the cadence. Compress to 14 days, not 30. Front-load the call attempts — three in the first four days, not three in the first week. Skip the extended drip sequence and replace it with one strong follow-up email sequence in days 5 to 10.

Where term life aged leads most consistently pay off: multi-product agencies that treat the term policy as the entry product. A 35-year-old with a term inquiry today is a candidate for IUL, disability income, or mortgage protection in two to four years. Close the term, start the relationship, convert it later. The CPA math on the initial term sale may be modest — depending on face amount and carrier, term FYCs run $400 to $800 at 20-year term for a healthy 35-year-old. But the lifetime value math on a multi-product relationship is a different calculation.

## The IUL cadence: extend to 60 days

Indexed universal life is consultative in a way that final expense and term are not. A prospect exploring IUL is typically evaluating a complex financial product — tax-advantaged retirement income, death benefit, cash value accumulation — and has questions that don't resolve in one call.

The IUL cadence isn't a pressure sequence. It's a meeting-building sequence.

Days 1 to 7: Two call attempts. The goal is a 20-minute discovery call, not a close. If you reach someone, your opener is about uncovering their retirement planning question, not pitching a product. If you don't reach them, email with a specific value proposition: "I help clients looking at tax-advantaged life insurance figure out whether an IUL makes sense compared to a Roth IRA or a 401(k). If that's a question you're exploring, I have 20 minutes."

Days 8 to 30: Educational email sequence. Three to four emails over this period, each addressing a common IUL misconception or evaluation question. This is where most agents underspend — sending one follow-up email and then giving up on a $5,000 commission opportunity. The IUL sale usually takes three to five interactions before someone is ready to look at an illustration.

Days 30 to 60: Proposal follow-through. If you've had a discovery call, send the illustration in week 4 to 5. Follow up once per week for three weeks. IUL aged leads that reach the illustration stage close at rates that make the $3 to $5 per-record cost essentially invisible in the CPA math.

## Compliance: three operating modes, your choice to make

Every agent calling aged life insurance leads in 2026 is making a choice about how they operate. The three modes aren't a spectrum of aggression — they're a spectrum of exposure management.

**Mode A (Conservative).** Manual dial only. No auto-dialer, no predictive dialing. Open every call with a reference to the original inquiry: "You reached out for some information on life insurance — I'm just following up." Keep records of your DNC scrub dates and list sources. Opt out immediately on request and update your suppression list within 24 hours. This is the right starting position for any agent unfamiliar with their specific state exposure, and it's the only mode that's defensible across all 50 states including FL, WA, OK, MD, and TX.

**Mode B (Operator Standard).** Manual power dialer permissible where state law allows. Verified consent documentation from the lead source. DNC scrubbed at purchase and at 30-day intervals. Documented opt-out protocol with same-day suppression. This works for most operators running aged-internet-form inventory where the original form-fill included proper TCPA disclosure language. Confirm with your lead vendor that the original consent documentation is available — if they can't produce it, operate in Mode A.

**Mode C.** Autodialer without confirmed written consent. Not recommended. The FCC's one-to-one consent rule was vacated in January 2025 and formally repealed in August 2025 — which removed a significant consent burden at the federal level — but state mini-TCPA laws in Florida, Washington, Oklahoma, Maryland, and Texas impose requirements that make autodialing aged-form leads without robust consent records high-exposure in those markets. The revocation-of-consent-for-all rule has also been delayed to January 2027, which changes the operational calculus for agents running established lists. If you're running significant volume in those states, the compliance picture is specific enough to warrant a consultation. [Henson Legal](https://www.henson-legal.com/) is our recommended TCPA counsel for aged-lead dialing programs.

The simplest rule for anyone starting out: manual dial, reference the original inquiry, stop immediately if someone says stop. That's Mode A, and it's where you build the conversion data to justify more sophisticated operations later.

## The four numbers that tell you if the cadence is working

At the end of 30 days across any batch, measure these four KPIs.

**Contact rate.** Contacts divided by dialable records (after bad-data scrub). Target: 10 to 15 percent for aged 31 to 85 day FE leads. If you're under 8 percent, your time-of-day distribution is off or your bad-data scrub was incomplete. If you're over 18 percent, you either have unusually fresh inventory or your scrub criteria were too loose.

**Appointment set rate from contacts.** Appointments set divided by contacts. Target: 20 to 30 percent for FE. Lower for term (15 to 25 percent) and IUL (10 to 20 percent, because the bar to "appointment" is higher). If you're under 15 percent on FE contacts, the opener isn't working — the most common fix is removing the product pitch from the first 60 seconds.

**Close rate from appointment.** Policies bound divided by appointments kept. Target: 40 to 55 percent for FE, 30 to 45 percent for term, 20 to 35 percent for IUL. Close rates below these ranges usually point to a product-fit issue (selling FEX to consumers who need term) or a presentation issue, not a lead-quality issue.

**CPA.** Total lead spend divided by closed policies. Compare against your product's commission ceiling. For final expense at $500 average FYC, a CPA over $200 should trigger a diagnostic. For IUL at $5,000 average FYC, a CPA under $2,000 is excellent. The numbers are structurally different; don't compare CPA across sub-verticals without adjusting for commission ceiling.

## What this means for you

Aged life insurance leads are not a volume-only game, and they're not a hope-and-dial game. They're a system game — and the system is the cadence.

A few things to put in place before your next batch:

- Segment your records by sub-vertical and lead age bracket before you dial a single record. The cadence is different for FEX, term, and IUL; running them the same way is what produces the "aged leads don't work" conclusion.
- Bad-data scrub on day one, before the first call. Returns are your revenue protection — track every invalid record and submit returns within your provider's policy window.
- Run at least 500 records before drawing any conclusions about conversion rate. Smaller batches produce variance that looks like signal but isn't.
- Use the 30/14/60-day cadence templates above as your baseline, then modify them based on your own contact-rate and appointment-rate data after the first full batch.
- If you're in Florida, Washington, or Texas, confirm state-specific compliance requirements before you dial anything. The federal TCPA floor still applies, but it's not the ceiling in those states.
