# Aged Life Insurance Leads: The Complete 2026 Operator's Guide

Every life insurance agent eventually runs the same mental calculation. Real-time exclusive leads run $50–$75 for term, $30–$50 for final expense, $40–$75 for IUL. The contact rate is strong on day one — and then half the agents who bought the same lead data re-enter the queue. By the time you're calling, you're competing against three other agents who got there first.

Aged life insurance leads flip that math. The same consumer data, generated from the same online quote forms, delivered after the initial auction window at $1–$15 per record depending on sub-market and age bracket. The contact rate is lower per call. The competition is near zero. And the underlying need — a mortgage to cover, a spouse to protect, a tax-efficient retirement plan — doesn't expire on a 90-day timer.

This guide is for the working agent or small-team operator who wants to evaluate aged life leads honestly: what they are, where the math works, where it doesn't, and how to build a cadence that extracts real value from the inventory.

## Why life insurance ages better than most verticals

Not all lead types age the same way. An aged auto insurance lead has a problem — auto policies renew every six months, which means the prospect either bought already or stopped shopping. An aged mortgage purchase lead has an expiry: the consumer found a house or stopped looking.

Life insurance is structurally different. The underlying need is long-duration:

- A 35-year-old with a new mortgage and two kids doesn't stop needing term coverage because three agents called in April
- A 64-year-old exploring final expense isn't going to stop being 64 because six months passed
- A high-income earner looking at IUL for tax-deferred accumulation is typically in a months-long research phase

The implication is real: a 180-day-old final expense lead is still the same age. A six-month-old IUL lead is still doing the same math. This is why aged life insurance leads at the older age tiers — 86–365 days — maintain better conversion value than aged auto or short-duration mortgage leads at similar ages.

The other factor is complexity. Life insurance products require education before purchase. Most real-time leads are contacted before they're ready, overwhelmed by competing agents, and then ignored. The aged version of that same lead has had time to think — and is often more receptive to a patient, consultative call than they were when five agents were blowing up their phone the week they submitted the form.

## Three sub-markets, three different calculations

"Life insurance leads" is a shorthand that covers three distinct markets, each with its own pricing range, commission structure, and case for aged inventory. Getting this wrong — buying term leads when you sell IUL, or treating FE lead economics like term — is one of the more expensive mistakes in this business.

**Term life.** Mid-market product. Monthly premiums run $20–$150 for standard applicants; first-year commissions (FYC) are $400–$2,000 depending on face amount and carrier. It's a volume model. Real-time exclusive term leads run $50–$75. Aged term leads, 31–85 days, run $5–$15. The commission ceiling is low enough that this is the tightest sub-market to make work on real-time inventory — which is exactly why the aged discount matters. At $8 per aged term lead versus $60 real-time, your allowable CPA budget stretches 7.5x.

**Final expense.** The senior market. Monthly premiums $30–$80, FYC $500–$1,500, most carriers paying 110–120% target premium first year on smaller whole-life policies. FE is primarily a telesales model. Real-time internet-form FE leads run $15–$30 shared, $30–$50 exclusive. Aged FE internet forms run $0.62–$1.88 for 31–85 days; direct-mail response aged FE runs $8–$25 exclusive. Two very different price tiers, two different conversion profiles.

**IUL.** The high-net-worth segment. Monthly premiums start at $300 and run past $1,000. FYC is $3,000–$10,000+ per closed case. The sales cycle is long — multi-call, multi-week, often involving a written proposal and a formal illustration. Real-time IUL leads run $40–$75. Aged IUL leads, 31–85 days, run $2–$6. The commission ceiling is high enough that both models can work. The key variable for aged IUL is whether you have the sales infrastructure to run a multi-touch consultative cycle — if you do, the aged discount makes this the most profitable aged lead sub-market in the entire life vertical.

## The math — two final expense agents, same $1,500 budget

Agent A buys real-time FE leads. $35 per lead, shared. Budget: $1,500. Leads purchased: 42.

Contact rate on shared real-time FE: 35%. That's 15 conversations — assuming you're calling in competition with two to four other agents who received the same lead the same day.

Conversion rate on contacted leads: 12%. That's 1.8 closed cases. At $900 FYC average: $1,620 gross on $1,500 spent. $120 net before overhead. Most months, Agent A breaks even or loses ground.

Agent B buys aged 31–85 day FE internet-form leads. $1.25 per lead. Budget: $1,500. Leads purchased: 1,200.

Contact rate on aged FE: 18% cumulative over a 10-day, six-touch cadence (three calls, two texts, one email). That's 216 conversations. No competition — nobody else is calling these records.

Conversion rate on contacted aged leads: 6%. That's 13 closed cases. At $900 FYC: $11,700 gross on $1,500 spent. Net before overhead: $10,200.

Read those numbers again.

The contact rate is lower on aged leads — that's not a secret and Agent B built a cadence that accounts for it. But the volume is so much higher per dollar spent that the total conversation count isn't even comparable. And because Agent B is the only caller on the record, there's no speed-to-lead race — just patient, consultative follow-up with a prospect who's already researched the product.

The math here isn't unusual. It's the standard case for aged FE leads run with the right cadence.

## What "the right cadence" actually means

The single biggest variable between agents who see the above numbers and agents who don't is cadence discipline.

Aged life leads require a multi-touch approach. Expecting to load 1,200 records, call each one once, and close 6% is a fantasy. The realistic contact rate per single-call attempt on aged leads is 6–8%. The cumulative contact rate over a proper sequence is 18–22%. The difference is the sequence.

A workable 10-day cadence for aged FE and term leads:

- Day 1, 10 AM: First call, no voicemail
- Day 1, 2 PM: Second call, voicemail (simple: "I saw you were looking into [product type] — I wanted to follow up")
- Day 2: Text message, simple (not automated-sounding: "This is [Name] from [Company] — I called yesterday about your life insurance question. Happy to talk at your convenience")
- Day 4: Third call, voicemail
- Day 7: Email (personal tone, brief, one question: "Are you still thinking through [product]?")
- Day 10: Final call, final voicemail

Six touches over ten days. That's the range where 80% of your contacts will happen if they're going to happen at all. Beyond day 10, you're working the long tail — move those records to a monthly nurture sequence and move on.

For IUL leads, extend the cadence. The sales cycle is longer; an 8-touch sequence over 21 days is more appropriate. IUL prospects are also more likely to respond to email — build your email sequence around value (an IUL illustration, a retirement income comparison) rather than "just checking in" follow-ups.

## Filtering decisions that change your numbers

The quality of your aged life lead campaign is partly the leads and mostly the filtering. Three decisions that move your effective contact rate significantly:

**Lead age bracket.** For FE and term, 31–85 days is the sweet spot. Contact rate is still meaningfully higher than 86–365 day leads, and pricing is substantially lower than real-time. IUL is different — the longer decision cycle means 86–180 day IUL leads still convert well, and the price discount at that age tier is dramatic.

**State filtering.** Contact rates vary by state. States with high cell phone penetration and low DNC registration density outperform others on raw contact rate. High-regulation states (FL, CA, NY) add compliance complexity. For FE specifically, avoid FL leads unless you're fully current on FTSA compliance — the Florida Telephone Solicitation Act applies additional restrictions to senior-demographic calls.

**Consumer age range.** For final expense, filter to 50–80. For term, 25–55. For IUL, 35–60 with income proxies if your provider offers them. Filtering outside these ranges isn't illegal — it's just a lower-probability call.

## TCPA compliance — the non-alarmist version

Aged life insurance leads purchased from reputable providers carry original form-fill consent documentation. The consumer opted in on an insurance quote form that disclosed third-party contact. That original consent is what makes aged internet-form leads legally distinct from cold call data lists.

The compliance picture in 2026 is more manageable than it was two years ago. The FCC's 1:1 consent rule — which would have required per-agent consent for every lead — was vacated in January 2025 and fully repealed by August 2025. Revocation-of-consent-by-all rules have been delayed until January 2027.

What you do need to respect, regardless of federal posture: state mini-TCPAs. Florida (FTSA), Oklahoma (OTSA), Washington (HB 1497), and several others impose tighter restrictions on calls to consumers in those states. For FE agents specifically, the FL FTSA is worth a direct read — it applies additional consent requirements for calls to consumers 60+ and some attorneys actively patrol it.

Before scaling any aged life lead campaign, confirm with your lead provider that they furnish original consent documentation. Any legitimate aged internet-form provider should be able to provide this on request. ## Where to buy aged life insurance leads — what actually matters

There are roughly a dozen providers selling aged life insurance leads in volume. Three things separate the operators worth buying from:

**Transparent published pricing.** Most providers quote pricing only after a sales call. That makes comparison impossible without committing time to multiple sales conversations. The providers who publish pricing by vertical, lead age, and volume tier let you run the math before picking up the phone.

**Original consent documentation.** Ask before you buy. A legitimate aged internet-form lead includes the timestamp, IP address, and disclosure language from the original opt-in form. If a provider can't furnish this, you're buying list data, not form-fill leads — and the TCPA exposure is different.

**Return policy clarity.** Industry standard for aged leads is up to 20% credit for verifiable bad data (disconnected numbers, deceased indicators, obvious duplicates). Any provider who can't articulate their returns policy in writing before purchase is a risk.

## What this means for your budget

The practical starting point for an independent life insurance agent testing aged leads:

- **Budget**: $500–$1,000 for your first order. Enough to generate a real sample, not so much that you can't absorb the learning curve.
- **Sub-market**: Start with your primary product. If you sell final expense, start with aged FE. Don't test three sub-markets simultaneously — the variables are too many.
- **Filtering**: State-specific (your licensed states), consumer age appropriate for your product, lead age 31–85 days for first test.
- **Cadence**: Six touches over 10 days, minimum. Less than that isn't a test — it's a pass.
- **KPIs**: Track contact rate, cost per conversation, cost per quoted, cost per bound. Not just "how many did I close." The diagnostic is in the ratios.
- **Scale trigger**: If your cost per contact is inside $12–$15 for FE and your cost per bound is inside $250–$400, you're inside a scalable model. If either number is outside that range, the problem is almost always filtering, cadence, or script — not the leads.

Run the 60-day numbers before deciding anything. One month of data is a sample. Two months is a campaign.

## What to take off the table

A few things that don't work the way some vendors present them:

Aged leads are not a one-call close channel — not for term, not for FE, not for IUL. If you're running a same-day close model built around real-time live transfers, aged leads require a workflow change before the economics will make sense.

The 18–22% contact rate is a cadence output, not a lead quality stat. Agents who load aged leads into a predictive dialer and run one-touch attempts report 6–8% contact and conclude the leads are bad. The leads are fine. The approach is wrong.

Aged FE internet-form leads and aged FE direct mail response leads are not the same product. The direct mail response lead is higher-intent and typically comes from a consumer who took an offline action (replied to a card). Contact rate is higher. Price is higher. For telesales operations with a strong one-call-close model, direct mail response aged FE is worth the premium. For agents building volume, internet-form aged FE at $0.62–$1.88 is the right starting point.
