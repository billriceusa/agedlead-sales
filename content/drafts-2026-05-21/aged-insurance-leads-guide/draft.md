# Aged Insurance Leads: The 2026 Buyer's Guide for Independent Agents and Brokers

Every insurance agent eventually asks the same question: real-time leads or aged leads? And the usual answer — "try both and see" — isn't useful if you haven't run the math first.

Here's the short version. Real-time insurance leads run $15–$75 depending on product and exclusivity. Aged insurance leads run $0.25–$3.00. The same consumer record — name, phone, original coverage request, household data — is available at 5% to 15% of the real-time price once it's been in inventory for 30 or more days. What changes is the freshness premium and the size of the calling herd. What doesn't change is the underlying intent.

The longer version is why this guide exists. "Aged insurance leads" covers four different markets — final expense, term life, health/ACA, and Medicare Advantage — each with its own pricing range, its own contact rate expectation, its own compliance frame, and its own answer to the only question that actually matters: what is your cost per closing?

## The four sub-markets — and why product type matters more than price

When an insurance agent asks about aged leads, the default assumption is that "insurance leads" is one market. It isn't.

**Final expense (burial insurance)** is a senior market. The consumer is typically 50–80 years old, owns a home, and expressed interest in a $5,000–$25,000 burial insurance policy. Final expense is sold door-to-door and by phone in roughly equal proportions. Aged final expense leads are the highest-converting aged insurance sub-market because the underlying problem — the prospect has no plan for end-of-life expenses — doesn't go away the way a rate-shop inquiry does. A 90-day-old final expense lead is still looking for a policy.

**Term life insurance** is a broader market. Consumer age ranges from 25 to 65. Policy amounts vary from $100,000 to $2 million. The shopping trigger is a life event — new home, new child, divorce, debt load — and those triggers are persistent. The risk in term aged leads is buying a batch that skews heavily toward one trigger type (e.g., mortgage-driven term) when rates have shifted significantly, which can lower re-engagement rates.

**Health/ACA marketplace** is an under-65 market tied tightly to enrollment windows — the main Open Enrollment Period runs November 1 through January 15 — plus year-round Special Enrollment Periods triggered by life events (job loss, marriage, birth, relocation). Aged ACA leads are most valuable in the 30–90 day range. A lead generated during OEP may still be uninsured or underinsured during the SEP calendar that follows.

**Medicare Advantage** is the most regulated sub-market in this group. The CMS TPMO (Third-Party Marketing Organization) consent rule that took effect October 1, 2024 changed the consent requirements for Medicare leads meaningfully. An aged Medicare Advantage lead purchased from a vendor who captured consent before October 2024 under the old standard may not have valid consent for an outbound dialing campaign under the current CMS framework. This is the single most important compliance issue in the insurance aged lead space right now, and it's why this category sits separately from Medicare Supplement, which is not subject to CMS marketing rules.

Medicare Supplement is regulated by state insurance commissioners, not CMS, and operates under the standard TCPA framework that applies to the other three sub-markets.

Real-time equivalents for reference: final expense at $15–$40, term life at $20–$50, ACA at $10–$30, Medicare Advantage at $40–$100+, Medicare Supplement at $25–$60.

The sticker-price gap is 10–40x. That gap exists because the real-time buyer paid a freshness premium for the right to call first. By the time the lead reaches the aged marketplace, two things have happened: the original buyer called once or twice and moved on, and the lead's cost basis has reset to zero.

The lead record itself — name, phone, product type, original coverage request — is the same. The competition has left the field.

## The math: two agents, same $1,200 budget

Let me show you what this looks like in the final expense market, because the numbers are representative.

**Agent A** buys real-time final expense leads. At $30 per lead, a $1,200 budget buys 40 leads. Real-time leads are sold to an average of 3–5 agents simultaneously. Effective contact rate on first dial: around 20–30% (the prospect is getting multiple calls in the first 48 hours and screening). Contact rate on follow-up dials after day 3 drops sharply because the original agents leave the lead and the prospect has moved on mentally. Say Agent A contacts 12 prospects over a 14-day cadence, converts at 8%, and closes 1 policy. Cost per closing: $1,200.

**Agent B** buys 31–85-day aged final expense leads at $1.50 per lead. Same $1,200 budget buys 800 leads. Aged final expense contact rates run 20–30% cumulative across a 14-day touch sequence — roughly the same as real-time, because the competition has cleared and the senior isn't screening calls from five agents simultaneously. Agent B contacts 200 prospects, converts at 5%, and closes 10 policies. Cost per closing: $120.

Same budget. Same product. Same market. The difference is the size of the calling herd and the math that follows from it.

Average commission per final expense policy runs $400–$700 at a $10,000 face amount. Agent A writes one policy for $450 gross minus $1,200 in leads: a $750 loss before expenses. Agent B writes ten policies for $4,500 gross minus $1,200 in leads: $3,300 gross profit before expenses. Volume changes everything.

## Where aged insurance leads come from

An aged insurance lead starts as a real-time submission. A consumer — searching for a final expense quote, a term life rate, an ACA plan comparison — fills out an internet form. That form submission is sold, usually within minutes, to 3–5 agents at real-time pricing. The agents who bought it call. Most call once, some call twice. By day 7–14, most have moved on to fresh inventory.

What they left behind is the lead record: same consumer data, same original intent, no more competition.

That record flows to the aged marketplace — vendors like [Aged Lead Store](https://agedleadstore.com/?utm_source=agedleadsales&utm_medium=affiliate&utm_campaign=aged-insurance-guide&utm_content=body-mention) aggregate unsold, un-worked, and post-campaign inventory from original lead sources and resell it in age-tiered batches. The price reflects the age tier, not the consumer's continued intent. The underlying problem — no burial insurance policy, no term coverage, no health plan — typically persists.

This is especially true in the senior markets. A 72-year-old who filled out a final expense form in January and was never reached is still 72, still uninsured for burial expenses, and still reachable in April with a modified approach.

## The compliance frame — TCPA and the CMS overlay

Aged insurance leads carry the consumer's original TCPA consent at the time of form fill. For manual dialing campaigns, the standard TCPA consent framework applies: verify that your provider has TCPA-compliant consent language attached to the lead, maintain a DNC list, honor opt-out requests within 10 business days.

For autodialing — automated dialer technology that uses a predictive or random/sequential number generator — the consent requirements are stricter. Your provider needs to document specific TCPA consent that permits autodialed calls, not just general marketing consent. Verify this before you run an aged lead campaign on an auto-dialer.

One note on the FCC 1:1 consent rule: it was vacated in January 2025 and formally repealed in August 2025. It does not currently create additional restrictions on aged inventory. But consent documentation still matters because some state mini-TCPAs (Florida's FTSA, Oklahoma's OTSA, Washington's HB 1497) impose requirements beyond the federal floor. Work with TCPA counsel on your specific calling program — [Henson Legal](https://www.henson-legal.com/) is the firm we recommend for insurance operators.

The Medicare Advantage exception is real and material. If you're working aged MA leads purchased before October 2024, review the CMS TPMO consent standards for your operation before dialing. ## Building your aged insurance lead operation

The mistake most agents make when they start buying aged leads is treating them like real-time leads with worse data. They're not. They're a volume play that requires a system.

Here's the structure that works:

**Cadence over speed.** Aged leads don't reward speed-to-lead — that's the real-time game. They reward persistence. A 14-day, 7-touch sequence covering phone, voicemail, text (where consent permits), and direct mail for final expense converts at 2–4x the rate of a single-pass calling campaign.

**Segment by product and age tier, not just age.** A batch of 31–85-day final expense leads is a different campaign than a batch of 86–180-day ACA leads. Script, opener, and urgency frame all shift. Work them in separate sequences.

**Match volume to your capacity.** An agent working a solo phone operation can typically run 500–1,000 aged leads per month at full cadence depth. Buying 3,000 records and running two dials per lead is worse than buying 800 and running a full 7-touch sequence. The math is in the depth of the cadence, not the width of the list.

**Track cost per closing, not cost per contact.** Contact rate tells you if your list is callable. Cost per closing tells you if the channel works. Target $100–$300 cost per closing in final expense, $200–$500 in term life, $150–$350 in ACA, depending on average commission per policy.

## Common mistakes that kill aged insurance lead campaigns

**Buying across too many product types in one batch.** A mix of auto, life, health, and final expense in one import looks efficient. It isn't. Each product requires a different opener, a different set of qualifying questions, and a different compliance frame. Agents who mix product types call with no clarity about why they're calling, and conversion tanks.

**Expecting real-time contact rates on the first dial.** You're calling day 31+. The prospect isn't waiting for your call. The cadence earns the contact rate — you won't get it in one pass.

**Not scrubbing DNC before each load.** The National DNC registry updates daily. A lead that was callable 60 days ago may be on the list today. DNC scrubbing is a pre-campaign step, not a one-time event. Services like BatchSkipTracing or DNC.com provide this.

**Working Medicare Advantage without reviewing the CMS consent basis.** See above. The risk here is real. If your lead vendor can't produce CMS-compliant TPMO consent documentation, skip MA and focus on MedSupp or final expense.

**Abandoning a cadence after two weeks.** Final expense especially has a long follow-up tail. Some of the highest-converting contacts come on touch 5 or 6. The agents who stop after three dials leave 40–50% of the conversion opportunity on the table.

## Practical takeaways

- Start with final expense or term life aged leads if you're new to the channel — simpler consent frame, straightforward product, highest-converting in the aged market.
- Budget $0.50–$2.00 per lead for your initial test batch (31–85-day age tier), target a $500–$1,000 minimum test volume to get statistically meaningful results.
- Build a 14-day, 7-touch cadence before you import your first lead — phone, voicemail, and text at minimum.
- Set your cost-per-closing target before you start so you know when the math is working.
- DNC scrub every batch before dialing.
- If you're working Medicare Advantage, verify CMS TPMO consent documentation with your lead vendor before you dial a single record.
- Track everything in a CRM from day one. Aged leads at volume without a CRM is a recipe for lost conversions and compliance exposure.
