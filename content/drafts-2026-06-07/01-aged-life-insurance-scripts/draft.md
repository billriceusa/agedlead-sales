# Aged Life Insurance Leads: Scripts, Cadence, and Sub-Vertical Strategies [2026]

Most guides on aged life insurance leads answer the buying question. They tell you the price, where to order, and roughly what conversion rate to expect. What they don't tell you is that term, final expense, and IUL are three completely different operations — and running the same script and cadence across all three is the fastest way to convince yourself aged leads don't work.

The decision tree starts here: which product do you sell? The answer changes your opening line, your cadence length, your call timing, your TCPA exposure profile, and your volume math. Get it right and aged life inventory becomes the most cost-efficient acquisition channel on your balance sheet. Get it wrong and you get three months of wasted budget and a spreadsheet full of unanswered calls.

## Why sub-vertical matters more than lead age

[The 2026 life insurance leads cost breakdown](/blog/life-insurance-leads-cost-2026) covers what aged life inventory costs by tier. This guide covers what to do after you download the CSV.

The working difference between the three sub-verticals comes down to two variables: the consumer's buying timeline and the natural decay of the original intent.

A final expense consumer who submitted an inquiry six weeks ago is still a senior who is still worried about burdening their family. The underlying need has a long shelf life. An IUL prospect who submitted a form two months ago is still a person who wants tax-advantaged retirement growth — that interest doesn't evaporate in 60 days. A term life consumer who submitted three months ago is a different story: they may have bought from the first agent who called, or may have decided the monthly premium isn't right for right now.

Understanding which need decays fast and which doesn't tells you where to put volume, how long to run a cadence, and what to say on the first call.

## Final expense — the high-volume telesales model

Final expense is the sub-vertical where aged leads are most widely used, because the economics are straightforward and the consumer's underlying need has genuine shelf life.

Aged FE internet-form leads run $0.62–$1.88 for the 31–85 day bracket. A structured telesales operation working 200 records a day can run a 21-day cadence that touches each record six to eight times across calls, voicemails, and texts. At those prices, the CPA math is forgiving — a 1% close rate on $1.25 leads produces a $125 CPA against a $600–$900 FE first-year commission.

**The FE opener that works:**

The most common mistake on aged FE is a question opener that recreates the original overwhelm: "Hi, I'm following up on your inquiry about life insurance, is this still something you're interested in?" That sentence puts the prospect on defense before you've said anything useful.

What works is a brief, benefit-focused re-introduction that doesn't spotlight the age of the record:

> "Hi, this is [Name] calling from [Agency]. You requested information a while back about burial insurance for your family. I'm not calling to pressure you — I just wanted to make sure you got what you needed. Do you have two minutes?"

The phrase "a while back" is intentional. It doesn't specify the exact date, which defuses any awkwardness about the gap. "Burial insurance" is the right vocabulary for the FE market — not "final expense" (industry jargon), not "life insurance" (too generic). It names the thing the consumer was actually researching.

If you get a conversation, ask one diagnostic question early: "Are you looking to cover burial and final expenses, or also to leave something behind for family?" The answer tells you whether to position a $10K policy or a $20K+ policy — and whether to lead with simplicity or legacy.

**FE 21-day cadence:**

- Day 1 AM: Call. Leave voicemail if no answer.
- Day 1 PM: Text (if cell), 3 lines: name, purpose, callback number.
- Day 3: Call, midday.
- Day 5: Call, afternoon. Voicemail if no answer.
- Day 8: Text reminder.
- Day 12: Call, evening (seniors are more reachable 5–7 PM).
- Day 16: Call and email.
- Day 21: Final call. Note result in CRM.

Non-contacts from Day 21 go to a 30-day re-contact file. Touch monthly for 90 more days. Experienced FE telesales agents close leads they first worked four and five months prior — the need doesn't go away.

**TCPA and the senior demographic:**

FE carries higher TCPA exposure than any other life sub-vertical because the consumer demographic is 60+. Florida's FTSA (Florida Telephone Solicitation Act) is the most aggressive state law in the country — it requires prior written agreement for any automated text or robocall to a Florida consumer, with no consent-transfer carve-outs. Oklahoma's OTSA and Washington's HB 1497 have similar provisions.

The FCC 1:1 consent rule was vacated in January 2025 and repealed in August 2025, but the state mini-TCPAs operate independently and are not affected by the federal rule change. If you're running auto-dialers into FL, OK, or WA, get jurisdiction-specific guidance from a TCPA attorney before you go live. [Henson Legal](https://www.henson-legal.com/) is the firm named in the Aged Lead Operator's System if you need a starting point.

**Mode A / Mode B operating posture for FE:** Mode A (conservative) means verified written consent before any autodialed or robotext contact, and no automated outreach into the high-risk state mini-TCPA jurisdictions without specific counsel review. Mode B (standard operator) means manual-dial cadence, scrubbed against the federal DNC every 31 days, consent records from the original form, but no auto-dial into FL, OK, or WA without counsel sign-off. Most independent FE telesales operations run Mode B on their core states and don't dial FL/OK/WA at all with automation. That's a defensible posture.

## Term life — the gateway product and the compressed cadence

Term life is the only sub-vertical where conversion decay is steep and measurable. A consumer who submitted a term quote request and didn't buy within the first 30 days has either bought from someone else, decided the premium doesn't fit right now, or moved on to a different product. Those things happen at higher rates in term than in FE or IUL.

That doesn't mean the lead is worthless. It means your opener has to acknowledge the possibility of changed circumstances rather than pretending no time has passed.

**The term opener:**

> "Hi, this is [Name] from [Agency]. You requested a term life quote a while back — I know sometimes situations change. Maybe you got it sorted out, maybe the timing wasn't right. I'm not assuming. Is this still on your radar, or did you get it handled?"

The second option — "or did you get it handled?" — is counterintuitive, but it works. It gives the prospect a face-saving exit. The ones who take it are done. The ones who don't say "actually, no, I never did" — and those are the conversations worth having.

**The multi-product angle:**

The real play with aged term is the multi-product conversation. Most people who bought a $500K 20-year term policy in their 30s are in their 40s now, reviewing coverage, carrying a mortgage, possibly now interested in permanent insurance or a conversion. Agents with a broader product shelf use aged term as the entry point for a life-needs review, not just a term close.

**Term 14-day cadence:**

Term buyers move fast or not at all. A compressed cadence is more efficient than a drawn-out one:

- Day 1 AM and PM: Two call attempts.
- Day 2: Text if cell number available.
- Day 5: Call with voicemail.
- Day 8: Second voicemail — different angle ("wanted to share one number that might change the picture for you").
- Day 14: Final call.

Three to five touches over 14 days is the right range for term. Extending to 21 days rarely adds proportional return because the conversion decay curve is steeper here than in FE or IUL.

## IUL — the consultative play where the math is most compelling

IUL is where the economics of aged leads become striking. A real-time exclusive IUL lead runs $40–$75. An aged IUL lead at 30–85 days runs $2–$6. The first-year commission on a typical IUL close is $5,000–$10,000. The math is so forgiving that a solo producer running a 30-day multi-touch cadence can build a $200K-gross book from aged IUL inventory without a speed-to-lead infrastructure.

But IUL is a different operation from FE. The consumer was researching a complex product — tax-deferred accumulation, retirement income flexibility, death benefit optionality. They were not in a crisis. They were planning. That changes everything about how you approach the first call.

- No urgency manufacturing. Don't try to "close" on the first call.
- Lead with education. "I noticed you were looking into IUL — most people I talk to have specific questions about how the indexing works or how it compares to a 401(k) after taxes. Do you still have those questions?"
- Expect three to four substantive conversations before a close. IUL sales cycles are longer than term or FE. The cadence has to accommodate that.

**IUL 30-day cadence:**

- Day 1: Call with voicemail introducing yourself as a resource, not a closer.
- Day 2: Email — brief, sharing a specific comparison (IUL vs. 401(k) after-tax income, indexed performance in a down year).
- Day 5: Call, second attempt.
- Day 9: Email addressing the most common IUL concern ("how the participation rate and cap work in a correction year").
- Day 14: Call, second voicemail if no answer.
- Day 20: Email — offer to build a free illustration for their age and risk profile.
- Day 28: Final call. Mention the illustration offer.

After 30 days, non-responding IUL prospects go to a monthly email-only file. IUL leads sometimes resurface three to six months later when a retirement conversation becomes more urgent — a layoff, a divorce, a 60th birthday. Keep them in the file.

## Filter settings that move close rates

Regardless of sub-vertical, the filters you apply at order time directly affect your results. The ones that matter:

**Age range.** FE: 55–80. Below 55, burial insurance is rarely the right fit; above 80, underwriting gets tight and conversations are harder. Term: 25–55. IUL: 35–60 — old enough to be planning retirement, young enough for permanent insurance to make actuarial sense.

**Lead age tier.** FE and IUL: the 31–85 day bracket is the core working tier. The 86–180 day bracket is worth testing for FE specifically — the underlying mortality concern doesn't decay the way term intent does. Term: prioritize 31–60 days; the 61–85 day cohort converts at a meaningfully lower rate.

**Product intent filter.** If your provider lets you pull FE-specific, IUL-specific, or term-specific records rather than generic "life insurance," take it. A 15–30% price premium for clean intent is almost always worth it. The conversations are shorter, the qualification is faster, and the close rate per contacted lead is materially higher.

**State.** License and appointment match is non-negotiable — carriers enforce this at commission time. For compliance: build your state list with your TCPA counsel before you deploy automated outreach, not after.

## What to do Monday morning

Pick one sub-vertical. Don't split budget across FE, term, and IUL until you have baseline conversion data in one of them. Order a test batch: 200–500 records, age-range filtered, your licensed states only.

Build the cadence before you load the records. Day 1–21 for FE, Day 1–14 for term, Day 1–30 for IUL. Track cost per contacted conversation and cost per application, not just close rate against records purchased. At 60 days, compare your CPA to the benchmarks in [the life insurance cost guide](/blog/life-insurance-leads-cost-2026) and adjust the tier, filter, or cadence before you scale.

The agents who struggle with aged life leads treat them like real-time leads: same script, same urgency, same cadence. The agents who make it work treat aged as a volume-plus-cadence operation — more records, more touches, product-specific openers, and patience with the natural conversion timeline of the sub-vertical.
