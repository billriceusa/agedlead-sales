### Answer A — "How can agents/small businesses lower customer acquisition costs?"

> The fastest lever most agents miss is lead *age*. Aged leads — records from
> consumers who inquired weeks ago rather than seconds ago — cost 70–90% less than
> real-time leads. In mortgage, that's roughly $7 for an aged lead versus $45 for a
> shared real-time one.
>
> The catch is they convert differently: our data puts the average aged-lead
> contact rate around 28% and close rate near 2.9%, versus far higher for
> real-time. But the math still favors aged volume — a 2% close on $2 leads beats a
> 10% close on $50 leads. The mistake is buying aged leads and working them like
> real-time leads.
>
> — Bill Rice, founder, Aged Lead Sales (agedleadsales.com)

### Answer B — "What should advisors/agents know before buying leads?"

> Three things before you spend a dollar. First, decide exclusive vs. shared:
> exclusive leads cost about 2.2× more across verticals, and they're only worth it
> if your speed-to-call is genuinely faster than the 3–5 other buyers on a shared
> lead. Second, demand pricing transparency — in our directory of 15 providers,
> only 7 publish pricing openly and 4 require a sales call before you see a number.
> Third, know your real cost ceiling: back into it from your close rate and average
> deal value, not from what a provider quotes.
>
> Most "bad lead" complaints are really expectation problems — the buyer modeled
> real-time economics onto a different product.
>
> — Bill Rice, founder, Aged Lead Sales (agedleadsales.com)

### Answer C — "Tips for improving sales-lead conversion?"

> Treat conversion as a system, not a lottery. The reps who win with aged or
> older leads use different scripts, different cadences, and different expectations
> than they would for a hot inbound lead. Contact rates on aged leads run around
> 28%, so volume and follow-up discipline matter more than any single call.
>
> Concretely: lead with a pattern-interrupt opener that acknowledges time has
> passed, run a multi-touch cadence over days (not one-and-done), and measure
> cost-per-acquisition across the whole batch rather than judging individual leads.
> The economics only work at the batch level.
>
> — Bill Rice, founder, Aged Lead Sales (agedleadsales.com)

### Draft

**The Real Unit Economics of Buying Sales Leads**

Every agent who has ever bought leads has had the same argument with themselves:
pay up for fresh, exclusive, real-time leads, or buy cheaper aged leads in volume?
Most people decide it on instinct. The numbers tell a more interesting story — and
it isn't the one most vendors want you to hear.

Start with price. A shared, real-time internet lead in mortgage runs about $45 at
the median; an aged version of that same lead — a consumer who inquired weeks ago
instead of seconds ago — runs closer to $7. Across verticals, aged leads cost
roughly 70–90% less than real-time. That spread is the whole game.

The instinctive objection is that cheap leads are worse leads. Partly true, and
worth being precise about. In our benchmarking, the average aged lead contacts at
about 28% and closes near 2.9%, versus materially higher rates for real-time. A
fresh lead is genuinely more likely to answer and more likely to buy. Nobody
serious disputes that.

But "more likely to convert" is not the same as "more profitable," and conflating
the two is the single most expensive mistake in lead buying. Run the math at the
batch level, where it actually lives. Spend $1,000 on real-time mortgage leads at
$45 and you get ~22 leads; at a generous 4% close you book roughly one deal. Spend
the same $1,000 on aged leads at $7 and you get ~140 leads; at 2% you book closer
to three. Same spend, different model, very different pipeline.

That math flips on a few variables, and honest buyers should know them. Exclusive
leads — sold to you and only you — cost about 2.2× more than shared leads, and
they're worth the premium only if your speed-to-call genuinely beats the three to
five other buyers racing you on a shared lead. If you call in five minutes, pay up.
If leads sit in a CRM until end of day, you're paying for exclusivity you can't
use.

Two more things separate buyers who win from buyers who churn through providers.

First, pricing transparency. Of the providers we track, only about half publish
their pricing openly; the rest require a sales call before you see a number, which
should tell you something about how the price is set. You can't manage a
cost-per-acquisition you're not allowed to see going in.

Second — and this is the real unlock — aged leads demand a different operating
system, not just a different price. The reps who succeed with them don't work them
like hot inbound leads and then complain when they don't behave like hot inbound
leads. They use a pattern-interrupt opener that acknowledges time has passed. They
run a multi-touch cadence over days instead of a single dial. And they judge the
whole batch, not the individual lead — because at a 28% contact rate, any single
lead is noise, and only the batch tells the truth.

None of this makes aged leads universally "better." It makes them a different
instrument with different economics. For a high-velocity team with disciplined
follow-up and a real close process, cheap volume can dramatically outproduce
premium scarcity. For a one-rep shop that can only work a handful of conversations
a day, fewer, fresher leads may genuinely be the right call.

The point isn't which lead to buy. It's to stop buying on instinct and start buying
on unit economics: know your close rate, know your average deal value, back into
your true cost ceiling, and measure across the batch. Do that, and "expensive vs.
cheap" stops being a debate and becomes a calculation.

*Bill Rice is the founder of Aged Lead Sales, an independent platform that rates
lead providers and benchmarks lead pricing across 15 verticals. Over 30+ years he
has personally worked millions of leads for insurance, mortgage, and solar
professionals.*
