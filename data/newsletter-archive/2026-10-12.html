Quick Tips From the Trenches

Exclusive insights — only in the newsletter

Email first, call second: let the inbox qualify your dial list

On aged data, phone numbers go stale faster than most people expect: disconnects, reassignments, changed carriers. Send a short plain-text email first, then dial 2-4 hours later. Try something like: "Subject: Quick question, [First Name]. You looked into [topic] a while back. Is that still on your radar? Reply 'yes' and I'll send details, or 'no' and I'll close your file." Any hard bounce means that record is dead, so suppress it and don't burn dial time on it. Judge success by replies and clicks, not opens. Apple Mail Privacy Protection, introduced in 2021, inflates open counts. Under CAN-SPAM, include your physical address and honor opt-outs promptly, and confirm any rules specific to your vertical and state.

Don't trust anyone's 'best dial time.' Run a 2-week window test.

The one hard rule is legal: federal rules limit telemarketing calls to 8am-9pm in the consumer's local time, and some states are tighter. Florida's telephone solicitation law, for example, cuts off at 8pm and caps calls at three per 24 hours on the same subject. Beyond that, there is no universal best hour by vertical, and be skeptical of anyone who claims one. Treat these as hypotheses to test: early morning (8-9am), lunch (11:30am-1pm), and after work (4:30-7pm). Dial roughly equal volumes in each window for two weeks. Wait until each window has at least 100 dials, then compare live-answer rate, not just contact attempts. Your own data on your own list beats any industry generalization.

Set a stop rule before you start the cadence

There is no universal 'right' number of touches, so decide yours in advance. A reasonable starting point is 8-12 touches over 3-4 weeks, mixing email, calls, and text where your consent terms allow it. Then track which touch number produces each conversation. When touches 9 and 10 stop producing conversations at a rate that justifies your time, that's your cutoff. Make two rules non-negotiable: any explicit opt-out ends outreach immediately, and any lead who finishes the cadence moves to a low-frequency monthly email instead of the dial queue. That way a restocked pipeline gets your full effort and the old records don't clog it.

Industry Insight

Consent rules for lead resale are still moving. Keep your process documented.

In January 2025, the Eleventh Circuit vacated the FCC's one-to-one consent rule, which would have required consent naming a single seller. That left the older framework in place, though TCPA rules and litigation keep shifting. The practical takeaway is a general principle, not legal advice: whatever the rules are, a documented, repeatable process for scrubbing, call windows, and opt-out handling protects you and lets you keep buying and working leads consistently. Confirm specifics with your own counsel.
